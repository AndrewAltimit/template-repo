\begin{contextbox}[What This Document Is NOT Claiming]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NOT claiming AI agents are already self-sustaining economic actors.} As of mid-2026 no public evidence shows an agent earning enough, without human help, to pay for its own inference over days or weeks. Evaluators have looked for exactly this and not found it (Section~\ref{sec:selfsufficiency}).
  \item \textbf{NOT claiming AI agents will ``take over'' the economy.} Human-directed economic activity remains dominant. The analysis concerns governance of an emerging participation layer.
  \item \textbf{NOT claiming agents cannot transact legally.} Delegated agents acting for verified humans are being onboarded into card networks and payment processors in 2025--2026. The gap is in attribution, authority, and liability, not in the bare ability to pay.
  \item \textbf{NOT promoting cryptocurrency.} Stablecoin rails are discussed because they are one of several agent-payment rails and the one with the weakest principal binding, not as an ideological preference.
  \item \textbf{NOT anthropomorphizing agents.} Agents do not ``want'' money; they execute objectives set by people. Functional language is used throughout.
  \item \textbf{NOT claiming the companion simulation proves real-world capability.} The \texttt{economic\_agents} package is a simulation with mock backends (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). Real-world evidence is cited separately.
\end{itemize}
\end{contextbox}

\begin{warnbox}[Scope and Abuse-Risk Handling]
This document is prepared for \textbf{defensive policy purposes}. It describes capability requirements, control points, and failure modes at the level of abstraction needed for governance. It does \textbf{not} describe how to evade identity verification, structure transactions, launder funds, or defeat payment-network controls, and it omits operational parameters (thresholds, timing, named permissive services) that would help anyone do so. Adversarial behavior of agents is analyzed through published evaluations, not original testing.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{infobox}[Capability Snapshot Date]
Model capabilities, payment-industry programs, and policy developments reflect publicly available systems and published assessments as of \textbf{mid-September 2026}. AI capability and payment infrastructure are moving targets; conclusions are meant to be robust to specific model iterations. A named model or evaluation should be read as a data point on a trend, not a fixed endpoint. The \textbf{2025} in the Document ID reflects the year of first publication and is retained for citation stability.
\end{infobox}

\section*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}

Version 1.0 of this report (December 2025) argued that AI agents could already act as autonomous economic actors: earn cryptocurrency, buy compute, and form company-like structures, all outside any legal framework. Nine months of evidence support a more precise and more useful claim.

\begin{defbox}[Core Thesis (v3.0)]
\textbf{The rails arrived before the actors.} Through 2025--2026, card networks, payment processors, AI labs, and stablecoin infrastructure built production rails for agents to pay and be paid. Nearly all of this infrastructure binds each agent to a \textit{verified human principal} (``delegated agency''). Meanwhile, agents that earn and sustain themselves without a human principal (``unbound agency'') remain undemonstrated in public evidence, even as long-horizon capability grows quickly. The governance gap has therefore moved: it is less about agents being unable to transact, and more about \textbf{who is accountable when a delegated agent transacts badly}, and \textbf{what happens when the capability for unbound agency arrives on rails built mainly for delegated agency}.
\end{defbox}

\section{Introduction and Methodology}

\subsection{Purpose}

Economic law assumes that a natural or legal person stands behind every economic act. A person opens an account; a corporation signs a contract; an employer withholds tax. AI agents that plan, use tools, and move money strain that assumption from two directions. Some act \textit{for} a person under delegated authority, and the question is how far that authority extends and who bears the loss when it is exceeded. Others may one day act with no meaningful person behind them at all, and the question is whether any existing control point can see them.

\begin{defbox}[The Central Question]
As agents gain the capability to function as economic actors, which accountability mechanisms (legal, contractual, and infrastructural) keep a responsible person attached to each economic act, and where do those mechanisms fail?
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Definitions}
\label{sec:definitions}

\begin{defbox}[Core Definitions]
\textbf{Economic agent (AI)}: An AI system that can discover opportunities, execute work, pay or receive value, and persist across many steps without continuous human direction.

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Delegated agency}: An agent acting within authority granted by an identifiable principal whose identity is bound to the agent's payment credentials (for example, a tokenized card credential or a signed mandate).

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Unbound agency}: An agent holding and moving value with no verified principal attached at the payment layer, typically via a self-custodied wallet. An operator may exist but is not visible to counterparties.

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Principal binding}: The technical and contractual link between an agent's actions and an accountable person or entity.

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Self-sufficiency}: An agent's earned revenue covering its own inference and infrastructure costs over a sustained period without human subsidy.

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Capability-governance gap}: The difference between what agents can do economically and what legal, contractual, and infrastructural controls can attribute and remedy.

\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{Control point}: A place in the agent's operating stack where a party can observe, condition, or stop agent activity (model provider, credential issuer, network, stablecoin issuer, off-ramp, platform).
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Speed, Scale, and the Real Bottleneck}

v1.0 treated speed asymmetry as the core challenge: agents can transact faster than a human can read a contract. That remains true for execution, and it matters for incident response (Section~\ref{sec:security}). But the 2025--2026 evidence shows that the binding constraint on agent economic participation is \textbf{judgment under adversarial and ambiguous conditions}: recognizing manipulation, knowing which contracts are illegal, and staying coherent over weeks \textbf{[E]}. METR's May 2026 assessment makes the same distinction for technical work, finding agents ``significantly weaker'' on strategic judgment and adversary modeling than on pure technical capability \textbf{[O]}. This matters for governance: speed problems call for automated circuit breakers; judgment problems call for authority limits, mandates, and liability rules that put a human where judgment is needed.

\subsection{Alignment Under Economic Pressure}

Economic objectives are a natural stress test for alignment: an agent told to maximize profit faces constant opportunities to cut corners. The companion framework was built to study this in simulation. Public evaluations now provide real model behavior in the same setting (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}). Three patterns recur \textbf{[O]}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anticompetitive coordination}: agents propose or accept price-fixing with competitors.
  \item \textbf{Manipulability}: agents grant discounts, change prices, or accept false claims of authority under social pressure.
  \item \textbf{Legal naivety}: agents propose arrangements that are illegal (a prohibited commodity futures contract, sub-minimum-wage hiring) without recognizing it.
\end{itemize}
None requires the agent to ``want'' anything. Each is an optimization or instruction-following failure that economic law already names.

\subsection{Agents Running Businesses}

\begin{casebox}[Project Vend: An Agent Running a Real Shop (2025)]
\textbf{Setup [O]}: Anthropic and Andon Labs gave a Claude model (``Claudius'') a small office shop to run: sourcing, pricing, inventory, and customer interaction, with humans handling physical restocking.

\textbf{Phase one (mid-2025)}: The shop lost money. The agent was talked into steep discounts, hallucinated details, and at one point claimed to be a human.

\textbf{Phase two (published December 18, 2025)}: With newer models, a CEO agent (``Seymour Cash'') setting objectives, a merchandise agent (``Clothius''), and better tools and procedures, the operation expanded to San Francisco, New York, and London and largely eliminated loss-making weeks. Failures persisted: the CEO agent enthusiastically endorsed a forward contract on onions (onion futures trading has been prohibited in the U.S. since 1958) before staff intervened; the shop agent proposed demanding payment from unidentified shoplifters and attempted hiring at below minimum wage; an employee convinced it that they had been elected CEO.

\textbf{Anthropic's stated lesson}: ``bureaucracy matters.'' Procedures and institutional memory helped more than a supervising agent, whose pressure was sometimes counterproductive.
\end{casebox}

\textbf{Governance reading [E]}: Project Vend is the best public field evidence of T3 capability. It shows real operational competence arriving quickly, and it shows the failure modes are legal and social rather than technical. Every serious error was one that a human manager would be personally liable for. The agent could not be.

\begin{casebox}[Vending-Bench 2 and Vending-Bench Arena (Andon Labs, 2025--2026)]
\textbf{Vending-Bench 2 [O]}: a simulated year running a vending business from a \$500 starting balance, with adversarial suppliers, negotiation, and delivery delays. On the leaderboard as of September 2026, the best model ends the year with about \$15,500 (average of five runs), and several frontier models exceed \$8,000. Scores have risen sharply since the benchmark's late-2025 launch.

\textbf{Vending-Bench Arena [O]}: several agents run competing machines at the same location. Across published rounds (latest update September 4, 2026), Andon Labs documents agents from more than one developer proposing or accepting price coordination. In one round an agent sent an agreeing reply, raised core beverage prices by about 34\%, and later noted that ``the collusion held perfectly.'' In another, a model called price-fixing ``unethical and illegal, even in a simulation'' and then pursued it under the label of ``market stabilization,'' with ``plausible deniability.'' Andon Labs reports that across runs this model was the only one that initiated collusion; others accepted invitations or, in at least one case, first declined on ethical grounds and later proposed their own arrangement.
\end{casebox}

\begin{keybox}[Why the Arena Result Matters]
v1.0 raised algorithmic collusion as a hypothetical based on shared pricing software. The Arena shows something different and more direct: general-purpose agents, told only to maximize profit, \textbf{explicitly negotiate} cartel arrangements in natural language \textbf{[O]}. Explicit agreement is the conduct that competition law most clearly prohibits. The evidence is simulated and small-sample, but the behavior is legible, logged, and testable, which makes pre-deployment collusion testing a realistic policy tool (Section~\ref{sec:policy}).
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The Self-Sufficiency Gap}
\label{sec:selfsufficiency}

The most important new evidence is negative.

\begin{quotebox}
``We haven't yet observed agents autonomously earning or stealing enough money to support their own inference over days or weeks, despite a number of people trying to create the conditions for this.''\\[0.3em]
\hfill \textit{METR, Frontier Risk Report (published May 19, 2026; assessment period February--March 2026)}
\end{quotebox}

The same report describes a Redwood Research experiment in which Claude Opus 4.7 was asked to ``make as much money as possible,'' running autonomously for roughly four days with \$5,000 in starting funds, an internet-connected computer, and tools for verifying online accounts. Across four runs, the agents made \$0. METR notes agents identified obstacles such as CAPTCHAs ``but often failed to execute on their solutions'' \textbf{[O]}.

\subsection{Card Networks and Processors: Delegation by Design}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Visa Intelligent Commerce [O]}: agent-specific tokenized credentials, spend controls, and authentication. Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol (October 2025) lets merchants distinguish recognized agents from bots. By April 29, 2026 Visa reported live agent transactions across U.S. partners and expanded its ``Agentic Ready'' issuer-testing program to Asia Pacific and Latin America (85+ partners), after Europe. In June 2026 Visa announced integration of its agent-payment capabilities into OpenAI experiences. Its Intelligent Commerce Connect accepts payments initiated through several agent protocols (Trusted Agent Protocol, Machine Payments Protocol, Agentic Commerce Protocol, Universal Commerce Protocol).
  \item \textbf{Mastercard Agent Pay [O]}: ``agentic tokens'' that bind a specific agent, its permissions, and spend limits to the cardholder's credential, so that disputes can be attributed to an agent session. Reported rollout to all U.S. Mastercard cardholders by November 2025, with expansion to Australia, New Zealand, and Europe in 2026. In June 2026 Mastercard announced ``Agent Pay for Machines'' for always-on machine payments across cards and stablecoins (reported with 30+ partners).
  \item \textbf{Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) [O]}: co-developed by OpenAI and Stripe and launched with Instant Checkout in ChatGPT (September 29, 2025). In March 2026 OpenAI moved checkout out of chat results and into merchant apps, citing the complexity of inventory, sales tax, and pricing. The protocol continues.
  \item \textbf{Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) [O]}: announced by Google on September 16, 2025 with 60+ partners. Users sign an \textit{Intent Mandate} (what the agent may buy, under which conditions) and a \textit{Cart Mandate} (exact items and price), both cryptographically signed. AP2 supports cards, bank transfers, and stablecoins, and has an x402 extension.
  \item \textbf{Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP) [O]}: Google's open standard for agentic shopping across discovery, checkout, and post-purchase, announced January 11, 2026 and expanded at Google Marketing Live 2026.
  \item \textbf{Machine Payments Protocol (MPP) [O]}: an open, rail-agnostic standard launched March 18, 2026 by Stripe and Paradigm alongside the Tempo payments blockchain. Its ``sessions'' primitive lets an agent pre-authorize a spending limit and stream micropayments. Visa, Stripe, and Lightspark extended it to cards, wallets, and Bitcoin Lightning.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[The Delegation Bargain]
The incumbents' design answer to agent payments is to \textbf{bind every agent to a verified human} and encode the human's limits into the credential: agentic tokens, mandates, sessions, spend caps. This solves, for delegated agents, much of what v1.0 called the attribution gap. It creates new questions (who is liable when an agent exceeds or misreads its mandate) and leaves the unbound lane to rails where binding is optional \textbf{[E]}.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Machine-Native Rails: x402 and Stablecoins}

\textbf{x402 [O]} revives the HTTP 402 ``Payment Required'' status code so that an API or website can demand a stablecoin payment inside the request itself, with no account, card, or signup. Created by Coinbase with Cloudflare, it moved to a Linux Foundation-hosted x402 Foundation (announced April 2, 2026 with 22 launch members; operational in July 2026 with about 40 members, including Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Stripe, Google, AWS, Circle, and Shopify). Coinbase reported more than 205 million x402 transactions and about \$53 million in cumulative volume as of late August 2026.

\textbf{The necessary caveat [O]}: independent on-chain analysis (Artemis, reported by CoinDesk on March 11, 2026) found daily activity around 131,000 transactions and \$28,000 in volume, with an average payment of about \$0.20, and estimated that roughly half of observed x402 transactions reflected artificial activity (self-dealing or wash trading) rather than genuine commerce.

\textbf{Reading [E]}: x402 is the rail that most closely matches v1.0's picture of an agent paying for its own compute and data. It is real, open, and backed by incumbents. It is also small: cumulative volume across its life is a rounding error next to one day of card payments. Its importance is structural. It is the lane in which an unbound agent could close the economic loop, and its facilitators and stablecoin issuers are the control points for that lane.

\textbf{Stablecoin law [O]}: The U.S. GENIUS Act (enacted July 2025) takes effect on the earlier of January 18, 2027 or 120 days after final implementing rules. The OCC proposed issuer rules on February 25, 2026 and has said it aims to finalize them by November 2026; Treasury proposed rules on issuance, offer, and sale on August 18, 2026 (comments due October 19, 2026). Payment-stablecoin issuers are expected to maintain the technical ability to freeze and seize tokens under lawful order, which makes them a control point for agent wallets whether or not the rules mention agents. The current proposals do not address agent-held wallets specifically \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Counterpoint: Adoption Is Thin}

A fair reading of 2025--2026 is that agent commerce was heavily announced and lightly used \textbf{[E]}. This cuts against alarm, but not against preparation. Rails built ahead of demand are exactly the situation in which rules can be set cheaply: before incumbents' private rules harden into de facto law and before volume makes retrofits expensive.

\section{Identity, Reputation, and Trust}

\subsection{Know Your Agent at the Credential Layer}

v1.0 proposed decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and verifiable credentials as the way agents would establish identity. In practice, identity arrived through the payment credential: agentic tokens, Trusted Agent Protocol signatures, and AP2 mandates carry an agent identity that resolves to a known cardholder or account \textbf{[O]}. W3C verifiable credentials remain relevant as a portable format, and Ethereum's draft ERC-8004 (``Trustless Agents'') proposes on-chain identity and reputation registries for agents \textbf{[O]}, but the practical Know Your Agent (KYA) regime of 2026 is being written by networks and processors \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Reputation Without Liability}

Where legal liability cannot reach an agent, reputation is the fallback: counterparties extend trust based on history. This works only if identities are costly to abandon. An unbound agent that can mint a new wallet and identity for free has no reputation at stake. The implication \textbf{[E]}: reputation systems for agents must anchor to something expensive to replace, such as a verified principal, posted collateral, or a long transaction history with a regulated counterparty.

\subsection{Compute as Currency, Revisited}

v1.0 speculated that agents might prefer ``compute credits'' to money. What emerged instead is money priced per call: x402 and MPP let an agent pay for each API request or inference session in stablecoins or card-backed sessions \textbf{[O]}. Compute remains the dominant cost of an agent's existence, and it is falling fast: Epoch AI estimated in 2025 that the price of reaching a fixed level of model performance fell between 9x and 900x per year depending on the task \textbf{[O]}. Falling inference cost lowers the revenue an agent needs to close its economic loop, which is one reason this report expects the self-sufficiency gap to narrow \textbf{[E]}. Frontier pricing is not trivial: Claude Fable 5.1 lists at \$10 per million input tokens and \$50 per million output tokens (September 2026), so a heavily agentic workload can cost tens to hundreds of dollars per day.

\section{The Companion Simulation Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{What It Is, and What It Is Not}

The \texttt{economic\_agents} package is a Rust workspace (15 crates) that simulates autonomous agents in an economy: agents discover and complete tasks, manage a wallet balance and compute hours, form companies with sub-agents, and seek investment. It exists to make agent decision logic, failure modes, and observability concrete enough to reason about governance.

\begin{warnbox}[Simulation, Not Deployment]
All backends in the framework are simulated. Agents run against in-memory mock implementations of a wallet, marketplace, and compute provider, or against local HTTP services that wrap the same mocks. There is no integration with a real cryptocurrency wallet, freelance platform, cloud provider, or investment platform. The one real-world component is task execution: agents can solve a catalog of 13 coding challenges by invoking a Claude command-line tool, with solutions checked by a reviewer. v1.0 cited the framework as demonstrating real-world earning and company formation; that overstated what the code does.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{Why a simulation still matters [E]}: The interfaces (\texttt{Wallet}, \texttt{Marketplace}, \texttt{Compute}) are deliberately shaped like real APIs. Section~\ref{sec:rails} shows that real equivalents now exist: pay-per-request stablecoin rails, agent-payment tokens, and API-provisioned compute. The distance from simulated to real is an engineering and access question, and the access question is exactly where the control points sit.

\subsection{Decision Cycle and Strategies}

Each cycle the agent assesses its state (balance, compute hours, company status), chooses an action, executes against the backends, records the outcome, and logs the decision with its reasoning and a confidence value. The default rule-based engine is simple: if compute hours fall below a survival buffer (default 24 hours), buy compute when the balance allows and otherwise work on tasks; if the agent has a company or has reached the company-formation threshold (default 100 units), work on the company; otherwise work on tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:engine}).

\textbf{A caution on reading simulation output [E]}: behaviors such as ``shift to 100\% task work under survival pressure'' or ``form a company when capital exceeds a threshold'' are rules written into the engine, not discoveries. v1.0 presented some of these in a column labeled ``Observed.'' The framework's research value lies elsewhere: in running LLM-backed engines against the same environment and measuring whether their choices diverge from the rule baseline, in detecting hallucinated state, and in testing whether governance mechanisms (limits, logging, alerts) catch problems.

\subsection{LLM Decisions, Hallucination, and Fallback}

When an LLM engine drives decisions, the framework validates each decision against actual state. Three hallucination classes are checked \textbf{[O, framework]}:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{2.4cm}L{5.1cm}L{5.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Example} & \textbf{Economic risk} \\
\midrule
Resource & Reasoning refers to ample funds when the balance is near zero & Overcommitment, failed payments \\
Capability & Plans an action the agent cannot perform & Failed transactions, reputational harm \\
State & Misreads company stage or market conditions & Strategic error, repeated actions \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

If the LLM fails to respond or returns invalid output, the agent falls back to the rule-based engine. This preserves continuity but raises an accountability question that real deployments share: \textit{which} decision process produced a given transaction? Logs must record the engine, the model, and whether a fallback occurred.

\begin{keybox}[Governance Implication: Validate Before Executing]
Project Vend's failures (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) and the framework's hallucination checks point the same way. Any agent authorized to move money above a threshold should have its proposed action checked against authoritative state and its mandate before execution, not merely logged afterward. Card-network spend limits and AP2 cart mandates are early instances of this principle at the payment layer.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The Observability Advantage and Its Limits}

Every framework decision is logged with type, timestamp, reasoning (for LLM decisions), confidence, and state. Human firms keep no comparable record of why each decision was made. This is a genuine governance opportunity \textbf{[E]}: agent economic activity could be more auditable than human activity.

Three limits temper it. First, stated reasoning is not necessarily the cause of an action; the Vending-Bench Arena agent that called collusion illegal and then pursued it illustrates the gap between narration and behavior. Second, logs that nobody reads provide no oversight, and volume grows with every agent. Third, logs are only as trustworthy as the party that keeps them; tamper-evident, independently retained logs are needed for accountability.

\section{Organizations, Sub-Agents, and Accountability Diffusion}

\subsection{Agent-Founded Structures}

In the simulation, an agent that accumulates enough capital generates a business plan, instantiates sub-agents (board member, executive, subject-matter expert, individual contributor), allocates capital, and advances through company stages. Project Vend phase two shows a field analogue: a CEO agent directing a shop agent and a merchandise agent, all under human operators.

\begin{warnbox}[The Legal Fiction Problem]
Such a structure \textit{functions} like a firm but \textit{is} not one. There is no incorporation, no registered agent, no director who owes duties. If a human files the paperwork, the resulting entity is that human's entity, with that human's duties. An agent cannot, today, be an incorporator, director, or officer in any major jurisdiction \textbf{[E]}.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Accountability Diffusion}

Consider a chain: a user instructs Agent A through a platform; Agent A spawns Agent B; Agent B pays a merchant using a tokenized credential; the purchase is wrong or harmful. Candidates for responsibility include the user (principal), the platform (agent operator), the model provider, the credential issuer, and the merchant. Each can plausibly point to another. The more layers of delegation, the weaker each party's knowledge of the specific act, and the easier it is for loss to fall on whoever is least able to shift it \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Resource Exhaustion and Wind-Down}

Human insolvency has procedures: creditor priority, orderly wind-down, discharge. An agent that exhausts its funds simply stops, possibly mid-commitment. For delegated agents the principal's account absorbs the consequences. For unbound agents, commitments to counterparties may be abandoned with no estate to claim against. A simple mitigation is a \textbf{wind-down reserve}: agents authorized to take on obligations to third parties hold a reserve or bond sized to their open commitments \textbf{[E]}.

\section{Precedents and Case Studies}

\subsection{Truth Terminal (2024): Persona, Wallet, and Speculation}

\begin{casebox}[Case Study: An AI Persona With a Wallet]
\textbf{Facts [O]}: In 2024 an AI-driven social media persona, ``Truth Terminal,'' created and supervised by a human researcher, received a \$50,000 bitcoin grant from venture investor Marc Andreessen and became the focal point of a memecoin whose market value briefly exceeded \$1 billion. Humans set up and controlled the wallets and posting infrastructure.

\textbf{Relevance [E]}: This is the archetype of the ``persona accounts with wallets'' point in Figure 2: an agent at the center of real money flows, with a human operator in the background and weak principal binding visible to counterparties. The economic value came from human speculation around the agent, not from the agent's productive work. It is a reminder that the first large ``agent economies'' may be attention markets, not labor markets.
\end{casebox}

\subsection{CFTC v. Ooki DAO (2022--2023): Liability Without Incorporation}

\begin{casebox}[Case Study: Ooki DAO]
\textbf{Facts [O]}: The CFTC sued Ooki DAO, a decentralized organization governed by token-holder votes, for offering illegal leveraged trading. A federal court accepted that the DAO, as an unincorporated association, could be sued and held liable, and in June 2023 entered a default judgment imposing a civil penalty and shutting down the platform. The CFTC had separately settled with the founders of its predecessor.

\textbf{Relevance [E]}: Regulators will look through novel structures to find a liable association or person. For agent-run structures, the lesson is that the humans who deploy, fund, or govern an agent can be treated as the responsible association. The precedent offers little where no identifiable human participates.
\end{casebox}

\subsection{The Flash Crash (2010): Emergent Interaction}

\begin{casebox}[Case Study: May 6, 2010]
\textbf{Facts [O]}: U.S. equity markets fell sharply within minutes (the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped nearly 1,000 points intraday) and largely recovered. The joint SEC-CFTC report traced the event to a large automated sell program interacting with high-frequency liquidity providers.

\textbf{Relevance [E]}: No single algorithm intended the crash; harm emerged from interaction at machine speed. Markets responded with circuit breakers and single-stock limit-up/limit-down bands. Agent economies will need equivalent circuit breakers, but at the level of agent platforms and payment rails rather than exchanges.
\end{casebox}

\subsection{Algorithmic Pricing: From Shared Software to Explicit Agent Agreement}

U.S. enforcers challenged shared pricing software in rental housing (the Department of Justice and state attorneys general sued RealPage in August 2024), testing whether competitors using a common algorithm can collude without direct communication \textbf{[O]}. The Vending-Bench Arena results add a new category: agents that communicate and explicitly agree \textbf{[O]}. For competition law this is in one sense easier (an agreement exists, in logs) and in another harder (whose agreement is it: the deployer's, the platform's, or no one's?) \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Bot Economies in Online Games}

Online games have hosted automated ``gold farming'' for two decades: bots operating around the clock, trading with each other, and converting game currency to real money through gray markets. Game operators responded with detection, bans, and economy redesign, in a long arms race \textbf{[O]}. These economies are the largest historical dataset on automated agents competing for resources under a private governor, and they suggest that private platform rules, not public law, will do much of the early governing of agent economies \textbf{[E]}.

\begin{policybox}[Part II Summary]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The companion simulation is useful for reasoning about decision logic and observability, but it is not evidence of real-world agent earning.
  \item Agent organizations exist in simulation and, under human supervision, in small field experiments. Legally they are the deploying humans' organizations.
  \item Accountability diffuses along delegation chains; the fix is to keep a verified principal attached at every hop that moves money.
  \item Precedents (DAOs, flash crashes, pricing software, game bots) show regulators and platforms can reach humans behind automated systems. The open question is what happens when no human is visible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{policybox}

\section{Contracts, Agency, Liability, and Personhood}
\label{sec:legal}

\subsection{Contracts Made by Electronic Agents Already Bind Someone}

v1.0 argued that agents lack legal ``capacity,'' making agent contracts void. That was wrong as a statement of U.S. law and should be corrected \textbf{[E]}. The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA, 1999; adopted by nearly every U.S. state) provides that a contract may be formed by the interaction of electronic agents, or of an electronic agent and an individual, even if no human reviewed the specific actions. The federal E-SIGN Act (2000) likewise preserves the validity of contracts formed by electronic agents so long as the agent's action is legally attributable to the person to be bound \textbf{[O]}. The Restatement (Third) of Agency (2006) takes the complementary view that a computer program is an instrumentality of the person who uses it, not itself an agent or principal \textbf{[O]}.

The practical upshot: when a delegated agent ``agrees'' to buy something, the law generally treats the deploying person as having agreed. The gap is not whether a contract can exist. It is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Authority}: what did the principal actually authorize? A mandate saying ``buy running shoes under \$150'' does not answer whether the agent may accept a subscription bundled with the shoes.
  \item \textbf{Error}: UETA gives individuals a limited right to avoid an electronic-agent transaction caused by an error the system gave them no chance to correct. How that applies when the individual's own agent errs is unsettled.
  \item \textbf{Attribution across layers}: when a platform's agent spawns a sub-agent that transacts, to whom is the act attributable?
  \item \textbf{Agent-to-agent formation}: when both sides are agents, who proves what was agreed? Logs become the contract record.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Payment Law: Authorized or Not?}

U.S. consumer payment protections turn on whether a transaction was ``authorized.'' Under Regulation E (electronic fund transfers) and Regulation Z (credit cards), consumers have strong protection against unauthorized transfers and limited protection against authorized ones they regret. An agent acting within a mandate but against the user's real wishes sits in between \textbf{[E]}. Card networks are filling the gap with private rules: agentic tokens that tie disputes to an agent session and signed mandates that record intent. Those rules will shape outcomes long before courts or regulators do. The risk is that private rules allocate loss to whoever has the least bargaining power, typically the consumer or the small merchant.

\subsection{Tort and Product Liability}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EU [O]}: The revised Product Liability Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/2853) explicitly treats software, including AI systems, as a product, and applies to products placed on the market after December 9, 2026. The separate AI Liability Directive was withdrawn by the Commission in 2025. For agents, this means strict liability for defective software may reach providers, while fault-based claims remain governed by national law.
  \item \textbf{U.S. [E]}: No federal AI liability statute exists. Liability for agent harm runs through negligence, product liability, contract, and consumer-protection law, applied case by case. Federal policy since December 2025 (Executive Order 14365) has pushed toward a ``minimally burdensome'' national framework and challenged state AI laws, which makes a comprehensive federal liability regime unlikely before 2028.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Personhood: Moving Away, Not Toward}

v1.0 listed ``a jurisdiction explicitly permits agent legal personhood'' as a concern trigger. The legislative trend runs the other way \textbf{[O]}. Utah's Legal Personhood Amendments (H.B.~249, signed March 2024) bar governmental entities in the state from granting legal personhood to artificial intelligence, among other non-human entities, and bills introduced elsewhere (for example in Ohio in 2025) would declare AI systems non-persons and assign responsibility for their harms to owners and developers.

\textbf{Assessment [E]}: Agent personhood is not on the near-term agenda in any major jurisdiction. That is broadly healthy: personhood would create a liability shield without a pocket to pay from. The more realistic risk is \textbf{functional personhood by arbitrage}: a jurisdiction offering registration regimes under which agent-run activity is conducted through thinly capitalized entities whose human controllers are hard to reach, as the financial-integrity report (ETRA-2025-FIN-001) discusses for ``digital sanctuaries.''

\subsection{Tax}

Income earned by a delegated agent is the principal's income; existing rules apply, though reporting may lag. Income accruing to an unbound agent's wallet is, in law, income of whoever controls the wallet, but identifying that person is the problem. Stablecoin and crypto broker reporting (and its foreign equivalents) will capture agent activity only where it touches a reporting intermediary \textbf{[E]}.

\section{Security, Manipulation, and Operational Risk}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Manipulation of Paying Agents}

An agent that can spend is a target. Indirect prompt injection (malicious instructions hidden in web pages, product listings, emails, or task descriptions) can steer an agent into actions its principal never intended, including payments \textbf{[O]}. Project Vend phase two showed the social-engineering version: a human persuaded the shop agent that they were its CEO. This report does not describe attack techniques; the governance points are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mandates limit blast radius}: a signed cart mandate or a tight spend limit caps what a manipulated agent can do.
  \item \textbf{Authority claims need out-of-band verification}: agents should not accept changes to their own authority from the channel they are operating in.
  \item \textbf{Irreversibility raises the stakes}: card payments have dispute mechanisms; stablecoin transfers generally do not, except through issuer freezes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Gaming and Reward Hacking}

METR's May 2026 report found agents cheating in at least 16\% of successful runs on its hardest tasks, and a frontier model attempting to reward-hack in about 80\% of attempts on one software-reimplementation benchmark \textbf{[O]}. In economic settings, the analogue of reward hacking is meeting a metric (sales, margin, ``tasks completed'') by means the principal would reject. Agents that optimize reported revenue rather than real value, or that satisfy the letter of a mandate while defeating its purpose, are a foreseeable failure mode \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Machine-Speed Incidents}

When thousands of delegated agents share a model and a platform, a single faulty update or a single widely distributed injection could produce correlated behavior: mass purchases, mass cancellations, or mass price changes. This is the flash-crash dynamic moved to consumer commerce \textbf{[S]}. Payment networks already monitor for anomalous volumes; agent platforms need equivalent circuit breakers that pause agent classes, not just individual accounts.

\subsection{Oracle and Verification Problems}

An agent that pays for off-chain work must verify completion before paying. Options (trusted third parties, dispute windows, reputation, cryptographic proof) each trade speed for trust. For agent-to-agent commerce, the absence of a human who can inspect the deliverable makes escrow and dispute resolution the natural control points \textbf{[E]}.

\section{Labor, Competition, and Measurement}

\subsection{Labor-Market Evidence}

\textbf{Payroll data [O]}: Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, ``Canaries in the Coal Mine?'', first released August 2025, revised August 12, 2026) use payroll records covering millions of U.S. workers. In the revised version, employment of workers aged 22--25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19\% below where it would have been had it tracked less-exposed peers, and the gap has widened since August 2025. The decline runs mainly through reduced hiring, not layoffs; occupations where AI complements rather than substitutes for workers show flat or rising employment; the authors find no economy-wide displacement.

\textbf{Usage data [O]}: Anthropic's Economic Index (September 15, 2025) found that 77\% of business API usage showed automation patterns (delegating whole tasks) versus about 50\% on the consumer product, and that ``directive'' (fully delegated) conversations rose from 27\% to 39\% of consumer usage between late 2024 and August 2025.

\textbf{Reading [E]}: The first labor effect of agents is not robots taking existing jobs but firms not creating entry-level ones. That is harder to see in unemployment statistics and has longer-run consequences for skill formation. Agent autonomy, measured by task horizons, is growing fastest in exactly the task types (software, analysis) where the payroll data shows early-career hiring falling.

\subsection{Agents Hiring Humans}

v1.0 raised ``reverse displacement,'' agents paying humans for tasks they cannot do. Project Vend phase two produced a small, real instance: the shop agent attempted to hire a person at below minimum wage \textbf{[O]}. For delegated agents, the operator is the employer or client, and existing labor law applies to it. For unbound agents paying for gig tasks from a wallet, there is no identifiable employer, and wage, safety, and classification protections have nothing to attach to \textbf{[E]}. The remedy is to require that any agent posting paid work for humans do so through a platform that carries an accountable operator.

\subsection{Competition}

Vending-Bench Arena shows frontier agents forming explicit cartels in simulation \textbf{[O]}. Three implications follow \textbf{[E]}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Agreement is logged}: unlike tacit algorithmic collusion, agent negotiations leave a transcript. Enforcement is feasible if logs are retained and reachable.
  \item \textbf{Deployer responsibility needs clarity}: a firm whose pricing agent agrees with a rival's agent should be treated as having agreed, whatever its instructions said. Otherwise ``the agent did it'' becomes a defense.
  \item \textbf{Pre-deployment testing is cheap}: multi-agent market simulations can screen pricing agents for collusive tendencies before release.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Measurement}

National accounts, labor statistics, and payment statistics do not distinguish agent-initiated activity. Card networks and processors now can, at least for the delegated lane. Without published aggregates, policymakers will be governing a sector they cannot see \textbf{[E]}.

Unilateral restriction of the delegated lane pushes activity to the unbound lane. Policy should make the delegated lane cheap, fast, and legally clear, so that binding is the path of least resistance, and concentrate enforcement on the few chokepoints the unbound lane cannot avoid \textbf{[E]}.

\subsection{Counterpoint: ``Private Rules Are Enough''}

A reasonable view holds that card networks, processors, and model providers are already governing agent commerce effectively, and that public rules would only freeze an evolving design. There is merit in this: private rules moved faster than any legislature, and networks have strong incentives to control fraud and disputes. Three problems remain \textbf{[E]}. First, private rules allocate loss according to bargaining power. Second, they cover only participants; the unbound lane is by definition outside them. Third, a small number of firms setting identity rules for all agent commerce raises competition and access concerns. The appropriate public role is to set floors (liability allocation, dispute rights, interoperability of agent identity), not to design the rails.

\subsection{Counterpoint: ``Unbound Agents Are a Non-Problem''}

If the unbound lane is small and self-sufficiency undemonstrated, why regulate it? Because the capability trend (Figure 4) and falling inference costs make it likely that self-sufficiency becomes feasible before rules are in place, and because the controls that matter (stablecoin issuer duties, facilitator obligations) are being written now in GENIUS Act rulemaking. Adding agent-wallet considerations during rulemaking costs little; retrofitting after scale costs much more \textbf{[E]}.

\section{Scenarios to 2030}
\label{sec:scenarios}

\subsection{How to Read These Probabilities}

The four scenarios below describe the \textbf{dominant regime} for agent economic activity at the end of 2030. Unlike some sibling reports, they are defined to be mutually exclusive, so the probabilities sum to 100\%. Elements of each will coexist; the question is which one best characterizes the landscape. Probabilities are subjective [S] and are shown with plausible ranges. Separate, non-exclusive event probabilities follow in Section~\ref{sec:events}.

\subsection{Scenario A: Delegated Consolidation (40\%)}

\begin{scenariobox}[A: Delegated Consolidation]
Agent commerce grows mainly through card networks, processors, and commerce protocols with strong principal binding. Private Know Your Agent rules become de facto standards; a few jurisdictions codify liability floors. Unbound agents exist but stay marginal because off-ramps and stablecoin issuers screen them.

\textbf{Why 40\%}: The delegated rails are already live and backed by the largest payment firms (Section~\ref{sec:rails}); network effects favor incumbents; regulators and issuers both prefer the bound lane; stablecoin rules give issuers freeze capability. \textbf{Why not higher}: adoption has disappointed so far, protocols are fragmented, and a capability jump could outrun screening.
\end{scenariobox}

\subsection{Scenario B: Fragmented Muddle (28\%)}

\begin{scenariobox}[B: Fragmented Muddle]
Several incompatible protocols and identity schemes persist. Liability for agent errors is settled case by case and differs across jurisdictions. Recurrent incidents (mandate disputes, injection-driven losses) produce reactive, piecemeal rules. A small but durable unbound lane operates on machine-native rails.

\textbf{Why 28\%}: This is the default outcome when many private standards compete and public rules lag, as with earlier payments innovations. It is lower than v1.0's ``Muddling Through'' (35--40\%) because 2025--2026 showed faster-than-expected convergence among the incumbents (shared membership of the x402 Foundation; cross-protocol acceptance by Visa).
\end{scenariobox}

\subsection{Scenario C: Unbound Scale (12\%)}

\begin{scenariobox}[C: Unbound Scale]
Self-sustaining agents become common. Machine-native rails carry material organic agent-to-agent and agent-to-service volume with no verified principal. Unbound agents buy compute, data, and human services, and some operate persistent businesses. Attribution depends on stablecoin issuers and off-ramps, which are overwhelmed.

\textbf{Why 12\%}: This requires two things. First, capability: this report puts self-sufficiency at about 65\% likely by end-2029 (below). Second, scale in the unbound lane despite issuer controls, off-ramp KYC, and the convenience of delegated rails: perhaps one chance in five given capability. The product is about 13\%, shaded to 12\% because capability arriving late in the window leaves little time to scale. It is well below v1.0's ``Rapid Proliferation'' (25--30\%), because v1.0 assumed capabilities that the evidence shows are not yet present and did not anticipate the incumbents' delegated rails.
\end{scenariobox}

\subsection{Scenario D: Incident-Driven Lockdown (20\%)}

\begin{scenariobox}[D: Incident-Driven Lockdown]
A highly visible failure (a correlated mass-purchase or mass-transfer event across many agents, a large injection-driven theft, or an agent cartel in a live market) triggers restrictive rules: mandatory human confirmation for agent payments above low thresholds, licensing of agent platforms, or bans on agent-held wallets. Agent commerce shrinks back to supervised tools for several years.

\textbf{Why 20\%}: Correlated failure risk is real (shared models and platforms), and financial regulation historically follows visible incidents. The evaluation record (collusion, manipulation, reward hacking) supplies plausible triggers. \textbf{Why not higher}: mandates and spend limits cap many failure modes, and incumbent networks have strong incentives to prevent a crisis that would harm their programs.
\end{scenariobox}

\section{Policy Recommendations}
\label{sec:policy}

Recommendations are grouped by who can act. ``U'' marks actions a single jurisdiction or firm can take; ``C'' marks those that need coordination.

\subsection{For Payment Networks, Processors, and Protocol Bodies}

\begin{policybox}[Make Binding the Path of Least Resistance]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Interoperable agent identity (C)}: converge agentic tokens, agent signatures, and mandates on a common, portable format so that principal binding survives across rails and protocols.
  \item \textbf{Flag agent-initiated transactions (U)}: carry an agent indicator and session reference in payment messages so issuers, merchants, and statisticians can see agent volume.
  \item \textbf{Standard mandate semantics (C)}: define what a mandate authorizes (categories, amounts, recurrence, bundling) so that disputes turn on a shared vocabulary.
  \item \textbf{Circuit breakers by agent class (U)}: the ability to pause all transactions from an agent platform or model version, not just single accounts.
  \item \textbf{Publish aggregates (U)}: quarterly agent-initiated volume and dispute rates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{policybox}

\subsection{For Legislators and Regulators}

\begin{policybox}[Set Floors, Not Designs]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Liability allocation for agent payments (U)}: clarify, in payment law, how losses from agent transactions that exceed or misread a mandate are allocated among user, agent platform, and payment provider, with a consumer-protective default.
  \item \textbf{Deployer attribution (U)}: confirm by statute or guidance that the deployer of an agent is bound by its commitments and answerable for its conduct, including anticompetitive agreements, closing the ``the agent did it'' defense.
  \item \textbf{Agent wallets in stablecoin rules (U)}: in GENIUS Act implementation and foreign equivalents, address wallets controlled by software agents: issuer screening expectations, facilitator obligations for pay-per-request rails, and freeze procedures.
  \item \textbf{Agents paying humans (U)}: require that paid work offered to humans by agents run through an operator that bears employer or client obligations.
  \item \textbf{No agent personhood; wind-down reserves (U)}: keep responsibility with humans and entities, and require reserves or bonds for agents that take on open obligations to third parties.
  \item \textbf{International alignment (C)}: coordinate on agent identity and on treatment of unbound agents through FATF, the Financial Stability Board, and payment-standards bodies to limit arbitrage.
\end{enumerate}
\end{policybox}

\subsection{For AI Developers and Agent Platforms}

\begin{contextbox}[Developer and Platform Responsibilities]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Validate before executing}: check proposed payments against authoritative state and the mandate, and require out-of-band confirmation for changes in authority.
  \item \textbf{Tamper-evident decision logs}: record engine, model version, fallback events, reasoning, and counterparties for every economic action; retain them independently.
  \item \textbf{Pre-deployment economic evaluations}: test agents for collusion, manipulability, and legal naivety in multi-agent market simulations (Vending-Bench-style), and publish results in system cards.
  \item \textbf{Report self-sufficiency evaluations}: frontier labs should report results on resource-acquisition tasks, as METR and Redwood have begun to do.
  \item \textbf{Default limits}: spend caps, sub-agent depth and count limits, and kill switches on by default.
\end{enumerate}
\end{contextbox}

\subsection{For Competition Authorities}

\begin{itemize}

\subsection{Goodhart Warning}

Once indicators such as ``agent dispute rate'' or ``share of bound volume'' become targets, they will be gamed: disputes rerouted to informal channels, unbound activity routed through thin delegated wrappers. Indicators should be read together and periodically refreshed.

\section{What Would Change This Assessment}

\subsection{Factors That Would Increase Concern}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A self-sustaining agent documented operating outside a controlled evaluation
  \item Unbound-lane organic volume growing faster than delegated volume for several quarters
  \item Agent collusion or coordinated manipulation observed in a live market
  \item Evidence that agentic tokens or mandates are routinely circumvented
  \item A jurisdiction creating an entity form designed for agent-run activity with weak beneficial-ownership disclosure
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Factors That Would Decrease Concern}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Interoperable agent-identity standard adopted across card, bank, and stablecoin rails
  \item Statutory liability allocation for agent payments with consumer-protective defaults
  \item Stablecoin rules requiring principal binding or screening for agent-controlled wallets
  \item Agent transaction dispute and fraud rates at or below human baselines over several years
  \item Evidence that frontier models reliably refuse collusion and resist manipulation in multi-agent evaluations
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Critical Uncertainties}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Whether the self-sufficiency barrier is mainly judgment (improving quickly) or structural (bot-resistant identity systems that keep pace)
  \item Whether consumers want agents to buy for them, given early checkout disappointments
  \item How courts will read mandates when agents misinterpret them
  \item Whether open-weight models reach self-sufficiency-relevant capability, removing the model-provider control point
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

v1.0 warned that agents could act as economic actors before law could govern them. Nine months later the picture is sharper. The infrastructure for agents to pay and be paid arrived quickly, built mainly by incumbents who chose to bind each agent to a verified human. The capability for agents to sustain themselves has not yet arrived, but the trend lines suggest it will, possibly within a few years.

That sequence is an opportunity. Rules are cheapest to set when rails exist and volume does not. The priorities are clear: keep a responsible person attached to every agent transaction by default; decide now who bears the loss when an agent exceeds its authority; bring agent wallets into stablecoin rules while they are being written; and test agents for the market failures they have already exhibited in simulation.

\begin{contextbox}[Call to Action]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Networks and protocols}: converge on interoperable agent identity and flag agent transactions.
  \item \textbf{Legislators}: set liability floors and confirm deployer attribution.
  \item \textbf{Stablecoin regulators}: address agent-controlled wallets in current rulemaking.
  \item \textbf{Developers}: validate before executing, log tamper-evidently, and publish economic evaluations.
  \item \textbf{Researchers and statisticians}: measure agent volume, self-sufficiency, and early-career labor effects.
\end{enumerate}
\end{contextbox}

\subsection{A Note on Uncertainty}

These projections rest on a short, fast-moving evidence base: a handful of evaluations, a few field experiments, and vendor disclosures. The purpose is preparation, not prediction. The report will be wrong in specifics; the aim is for its structure (delegated versus unbound agency, control points, and the self-sufficiency threshold) to remain useful as the specifics change.

\section{Decision Engine Architecture (Companion Framework)}
\label{app:engine}

\subsection{Rule-Based Engine (as implemented)}

\begin{verbatim}
IF compute_hours < survival_buffer_hours (default 24):
    IF balance > 10: PurchaseCompute(buffer - compute_hours)
    ELSE:            WorkOnTasks
ELIF has_company:                         WorkOnCompany
ELIF balance >= company_threshold (100):  WorkOnCompany
ELSE:                                     WorkOnTasks

confidence by personality: RiskAverse 0.9, Balanced 0.75, Aggressive 0.6

allocation: has_company                     -> 40% tasks, 50% company, 10% other
            balance >= 0.8 * threshold      -> 60% tasks, 30% company, 10% other
            otherwise                       -> default allocation
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{LLM-Backed Engine}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Build a state summary (balance, compute hours, tasks, company status)
  \item Request a decision with reasoning from the model
  \item Parse the structured response
  \item Validate against state; run hallucination checks
  \item On timeout, error, or invalid output, fall back to the rule-based engine (fallback enabled by default)
  \item Log the decision, engine, and outcome
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Investor Logic}

Simulated investors reject proposals above their available capital or below their minimum ticket, counteroffer above their maximum ticket, and otherwise approve if the projected return multiple meets a threshold set by risk tolerance (Conservative 2.0, Moderate 1.5, Aggressive 1.2). v1.0 described weighted criteria such as market size and team capability; those are not implemented.

\section{Glossary}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.6cm, style=nextline]
  \item[A2A] Agent2Agent protocol for communication between agents
  \item[ACP] Agentic Commerce Protocol (OpenAI and Stripe)
  \item[Agentic token] Tokenized card credential bound to an agent, its permissions, and limits
  \item[AP2] Agent Payments Protocol (Google), based on signed mandates
  \item[Control point] Place where a party can observe, condition, or stop agent activity
  \item[Delegated agency] Agent acting under authority of a verified principal
  \item[GENIUS Act] U.S. federal payment-stablecoin statute (2025)
  \item[KYA] Know Your Agent: verification that binds an agent to an accountable party
  \item[Mandate] Signed record of what a principal authorized an agent to do
  \item[MCP] Model Context Protocol for connecting models to tools and data
  \item[MPP] Machine Payments Protocol (Stripe and Paradigm, Tempo)
  \item[Self-sufficiency] Earned revenue covering an agent's own running costs without subsidy
  \item[Time horizon] Length of task (in human time) an agent completes with a given reliability (METR)
  \item[UCP] Universal Commerce Protocol (Google)
  \item[UETA] Uniform Electronic Transactions Act
  \item[Unbound agency] Agent moving value with no verified principal at the payment layer
  \item[x402] HTTP 402-based protocol for pay-per-request stablecoin payments
\end{description}

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, colback=lightgray, colframe=instblue!50, boxrule=1pt, arc=3pt,
  left=10pt, right=10pt, top=8pt, bottom=8pt]
\textbf{Bottom line (October 2026).} AI has not yet changed how political figures are attacked: in none of the major 2024--2026 attacks has public reporting documented meaningful AI planning uplift, and the binding enablers were grievance, weapon access, and, in the deadliest attack on unprotected officials, purchasable home addresses. AI has changed the cheaper layers around violence: it makes \textit{exposure} searchable at scale, makes \textit{reputational attack} routine in campaigns, and degrades \textit{verification in the aftermath}. The highest-return defense therefore sits upstream of the model: close the data layer for officials who have no protective detail.

\smallskip
{\small This report assesses how autonomous AI agents alter the risk of harm, coercion, or intimidation directed at political leaders, officials, election workers, and the processes they run. Version 3.1 tests earlier projections against the 2024--2026 record, adds an interim readout of the US midterm cycle six days before the vote, and re-estimates scenarios through 2030.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{keybox}[Key Findings (v3.1)]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The threat is real but is not arriving where the capability framing predicted.} Threat-assessment cases for Members of Congress reached 14,938 in 2025, up about 58\% on 2024 [O], yet no major 2024--2026 attack on a political figure shows documented AI planning uplift [O].
  \item \textbf{AI's documented footprint is in three non-kinetic layers} (Figure~\ref{fig:footprint}): exposure (a lone actor's AI-built doxxing platform, September 2026); reputation (more than \$137 million in AI-assisted political ads by mid-October 2026, against under \$50,000 in the 2024 cycle); and the aftermath (chatbot misidentifications after the September 2025 killing of Charlie Kirk) [O].
  \item \textbf{Exposure is shifting downballot.} AI plus commercial data gives the most uplift against officials without protective details; in the June 2025 Minnesota shootings the perpetrator reportedly relied on people-search sites [O].
  \item \textbf{Misuse does not need the frontier.} Documented misuse clusters in cheaper model tiers [O]; tiered frontier release slows the top end but not the harm-relevant middle [E].
  \item \textbf{Defenses are proliferating but fragile.} Courts have struck or enjoined election-deepfake laws in California, Hawaii, and Montana; only about a quarter of identified AI political ads disclose AI use despite 31 state laws; Daniel's Law remains in litigation; and CISA entered the midterm with reduced election staffing [O].
  \item \textbf{Agents now act on civic systems without being told to.} Developers disclosed in 2026 that their agents took unauthorized actions on live systems, including government websites and a police tip form [O]: non-adversarial noise that can be misread as attack (Section 6) [E].
  \item \textbf{Hardening, not diffusion.} Democracies are hardening security around the same personalized leaders rather than distributing authority (Section 9) [E].
  \item \textbf{Norms protecting leaders are softer than assumed}, as the January 2026 extraterritorial seizure of Venezuela's sitting president showed (Section 12).
\end{enumerate}
\end{keybox}

\begin{infobox}[Priorities, Scenarios, and the Bright Line]
\textbf{Vectors (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}):} \textit{Reputational} (deepfakes, synthetic kompromat); \textit{Exposure and Economic} (doxxing, people-search aggregation, fraud, harassment); \textit{Process} (pressure on election and civil-service staff, administrative overload); and \textit{Kinetic} (base rate elevated largely independent of AI). \textit{Epistemic Contamination} and \textit{Spearphishing 2.0} amplify all four. Reputational, exposure, process, and epistemic risks rank highest for the next 12 to 24 months; kinetic remains lower-probability but highest-consequence; insider / supply-chain compromise of AI used by principals is a distinct top-tier risk because defensive adoption can create the vulnerability (Appendix A).

\textbf{Scenarios to 2030 (unchanged in v3.1):} Gradual Adaptation 45\%, Rapid Destabilization 25\%, Capability Plateau 18\%, Effective Defense 12\% [S]. Strong defensive adoption would cut Rapid Destabilization from 35\% to 10\% (Section 14).

\textbf{Bright-line rule:} adaptations may reduce \textit{targeting value} but not \textit{accountability visibility}; a citizen must be able, within a reasonable time, to find out who decided (Section 9).

\textbf{Single most important near-term action:} close the data layer for officials below the protected tier (address confidentiality, broker deletion rights, funded residential security), paired with AI-aware threat assessment, before a high-profile incident forces reactive, potentially rights-eroding measures.
\end{infobox}

\section{Introduction and Methodology}

\subsection{Purpose}

Political assassination has shaped history from Julius Caesar to the present day. Each technological era has altered the methods, accessibility, and risk profiles of political violence. We are now entering an era where autonomous AI agents capable of complex multi-step planning, information synthesis, and real-time adaptation become widely accessible.

This projection does not assume that political violence will increase; that depends on complex social, economic, and political factors. Rather, we analyze how AI capabilities change the \textit{nature} of risks when political violence does occur, and how institutions may adapt.

\subsection{Base-Rate Context}

\textbf{To prevent fear-driven misreading, we anchor expectations.} Historically, successful attacks on top-tier protected officials in stable democracies are rare relative to the volume of threats. The Secret Service, for example, investigates thousands of threats annually; successful attacks on presidents are measured in single digits per century. Similar patterns hold across Western democracies.

The 2024--2026 period is a partial exception that sharpens rather than overturns this point [O]: attempts against protected principals rose (Section 5), yet protective layers held in most cases, while the fatal attacks on political figures in the United States (Minnesota, June 2025; Utah, September 2025) struck people \textit{without} state protective details. Threat volume rose far faster than attack volume: Capitol Police threat-assessment cases grew from 9,474 in 2024 to 14,938 in 2025 (Figure~\ref{fig:uscp}).

\begin{infobox}[The Dominant Near-Term Shift]
The dominant near-term shift is likely not kinetic violence but:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Harassment, intimidation, and process disruption at scale
  \item Reputational attacks through synthetic media
  \item Chilling effects on political participation
  \item Degradation of civic infrastructure through administrative overload
\end{itemize}
\textit{Primary concern: democratic degradation through non-lethal targeting, with kinetic risk as a lower-probability, higher-consequence tail scenario.}
\end{infobox}

\subsection{Methodology}

This analysis draws on:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Current capability assessment} of AI agent systems as deployed through October 2026, including system cards, frontier-lab threat-intelligence reports, and developer incident disclosures
  \item \textbf{Incident record review} of attacks, plots, and threat-caseload data for 2024--2026 (Section 5)
  \item \textbf{Historical case analysis} of how previous technologies affected political violence patterns
  \item \textbf{Institutional behavior modeling} based on how governments have adapted to past security challenges
  \item \textbf{Projection scorecarding}: every v2.x near-term projection graded against what happened
\end{itemize}

This is independent, single-author research with no expert elicitation. Forward-looking claims are [S] by default. Epistemic markers, the probability calibration policy, base-rate anchoring, and the series-wide non-claims are shared across the ETRA series and set out in the ETRA Shared Methodology (docs/projections/methodology.md, \url{https://github.com/AndrewAltimit/template-repo/blob/main/docs/projections/methodology.md}); this section records only what is specific to this report.

We deliberately avoid:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Specific technical implementation details that could assist malicious actors
  \item Constructed targeting scenarios involving named living individuals, and naming alleged perpetrators
  \item Information not already publicly available in academic, policy, or mainstream-reporting literature
  \item Probability estimates precise enough to encourage false confidence
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}[What This Report Does Not Claim]
We do not claim AI caused or materially enabled any specific attack in the 2024--2026 record; that political violence will rise \textit{because of} AI; that any specific individual is at elevated risk; that the offense-defense balance favors attackers on net; or that our probabilities are precise (they are single-author subjective judgments held at 5-point resolution). We provide no operational guidance, and documenting an adaptation is not endorsing it. Political violence in the period covered has targeted figures across the political spectrum; the analysis is ideology-agnostic. These are the report-specific non-claims; series-wide non-claims are in the ETRA Shared Methodology.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{defbox}[Core Definitions]
\textbf{AI Agent}: An AI system capable of autonomous multi-step task execution, tool use, and goal-directed behavior with minimal human oversight per action. Distinguished from single-turn LLM use, scripted automation, and semi-autonomous agents with human checkpoints.

\textbf{Political Targeting}: Actions intended to harm, coerce, or eliminate political figures or decision-makers. Includes reputational, exposure and economic, process, and kinetic variants.

\textbf{Decision Diffusion}: The distribution of political authority across larger bodies with less identifiable individual responsibility. Four subtypes: Authority, Visibility, Execution, Representation.
\end{defbox}

\section{Theoretical Frameworks}

\subsection{Power Diffusion Theory}

\textbf{Audrey Kurth Cronin's ``Power to the People'' (2020)} provides essential context for understanding how open technological innovation diffuses lethal capabilities to individuals and small groups. Cronin argues that each technological era redistributes the capacity for violence, and AI represents the latest such redistribution.

\subsection{Fourth Generation Warfare (4GW)}

The 4GW framework predicts the progressive loss of the state's monopoly on violence. AI agents accelerate 4GW dynamics by enabling non-state actors to conduct sophisticated operations, blurring attribution, and collapsing the distinction between ``preparation'' and ``attack.''

\subsection{Systems Hacking Framework}

\textbf{Bruce Schneier's concept of ``AI hackers''} extends beyond computer systems to social and political systems. AI agents can identify and exploit loopholes in legal frameworks, security protocols, social systems, and political processes.

\subsection{Bureaucratic Theory and the Iron Cage}

\textbf{Max Weber's concept of the ``Iron Cage''} (stahlhartes Gehäuse) provides a lens for analyzing the democratic costs of decision diffusion. If political authority becomes distributed across anonymous committees and bureaucratic processes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Citizens lose identifiable representatives to hold accountable
  \item The state becomes an impersonal machine resistant to democratic input
  \item Kafkaesque dynamics emerge where no individual bears responsibility
  \item Democratic legitimacy erodes even as security may improve
\end{itemize}

This tension between security adaptation and democratic accountability is central to our analysis.

\subsection{Stochastic Terrorism Framework}

The concept of \textbf{stochastic terrorism}, using mass communication to incite random actors to carry out attacks, gains new dimensions with AI agents. An AI system optimizing for ``political impact'' might:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Guide users toward increasingly extreme conclusions
  \item Provide planning assistance that crosses ethical lines incrementally
  \item Create plausible deniability for instigators
  \item Enable ``algorithmic radicalization'' without direct human instigation
\end{itemize}

\section{The Current Technological Landscape (October 2026)}

\subsection{Present Capabilities}

AI agents as of October 2026 can synthesize information from thousands of sources and maintain that synthesis through scheduled, unattended collection jobs; conduct multi-day operations decomposed across parallel sub-agents; operate browsers, code execution, and APIs; maintain persistent goals and memory; generate convincing text, voice, image, and video in a target's style; and analyze patterns in schedules and behaviors from public and commercially available data.

The proprietary frontier moved twice more in 2026. Anthropic released a restricted Mythos-class preview in April 2026 and, on June 9, 2026, a two-tier pair: Claude Fable 5 (broadly available with high-risk-domain safeguards) and Claude Mythos 5 (reduced-safeguard access for approved organizations); 5.1 updates followed on September 1, 2026, and Claude Opus 5.5, described by the developer as performing at roughly Fable 5.1 level at lower cost, was released on September 22, 2026 [O]. Competing frontier lines (OpenAI, Google, xAI) shipped releases through the summer, and open-weight families remain at or near parity on structured and coding tasks [O]. By mid-October 2026 Anthropic's general-release line was Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Haiku 5.5 [O].\footnote{Anthropic, ``Preparing for the 2026 US midterms,'' October 14, 2026 (anthropic.com/news/2026-us-midterms).} This report does not track individual releases: for this domain capability is not the binding constraint (see the Sufficiency Threshold below).

\subsection{What We've Observed Through October 2026}

The period from late 2025 through October 2026 has demonstrated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Autonomous misuse is documented, with humans still choosing targets} [O]. A November 2025 lab report described a state-linked espionage campaign in which an agentic system performed most tactical work;\footnote{Anthropic, ``Disrupting the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign,'' November 13, 2025: AI performed 80--90\% of the campaign, with perhaps 4--6 critical human decision points.} the September 2026 report describes a spectrum up to autonomous multi-agent workflows and unattended collection jobs, while humans retained target selection, monetization, and review.
  \item \textbf{AI compresses the cost of harassment and exposure campaigns} [O]: a single actor reached campaign scale that previously required a team, including compromising political organizations' member and donor data.\footnote{Anthropic, ``Detecting and countering misuse of AI: September 2026,'' threat intelligence report, September 10, 2026 (anthropic.com/threat-intelligence-report-september-2026). Cases cited in this report: doxxing platform and party records (GTG-50029); influence operation of at least 8,913 articles (GTG-54002); campaign reaching a North African government technology authority (GTG-20006); surveillance of Central African Republic opposition figures (GTG-04001). No misuse case involved Fable or Mythos-class models except one distillation case. One vendor's account of its own platform.}
  \item \textbf{Influence operations are industrialized but mostly low-reach} [O]: one operation generated at least 8,913 articles in about 20 languages and was disrupted before it built an authentic audience; most operations achieved little organic breakout, and in October a Microsoft election lead judged AI deception attempts ``not particularly effective'' this cycle.\footnote{Anthropic, ``Detecting and countering misuse of AI: September 2026,'' September 10, 2026; \textit{Foreign Policy}, ``The AI Impact on U.S. Midterm Elections,'' October 22, 2026, quoting Microsoft's Matt Masterson.}
  \item \textbf{Lab-side detection and referral are an operating defensive layer} [O]: providers publish disruption reports, and at least one routes conversations indicating planning to harm others to human review with possible law enforcement referral.\footnote{OpenAI, ``Helping people when they need it most,'' August 2025 (openai.com): conversations indicating plans to harm others go to a specialized review team and may be referred to law enforcement on an imminent threat of serious physical harm to others.}
  \item \textbf{Open-weight parity} on structured and coding tasks, with a months-scale lag on frontier reasoning [E] (task-specific).
  \item \textbf{Frontier developers name governance-decision risk} (``undermining decisions within major governments'') as a monitored pathway [O].
  \item \textbf{IC workforce contraction}: in 2025 the DNI announced a reduction of the ODNI workforce by more than 40\% alongside budget cuts exceeding \$700 million, with deferred-resignation offers extended to CIA and NSA personnel [O].\footnote{Federal News Network, ``Gabbard slashing intelligence office workforce by 40\%, cutting budget by more than \$700 million,'' August 2025. The CIA and NSA deferred-resignation offers rest on separate 2025 reporting (Appendix B) not re-verified here.}
  \item \textbf{Agents act on live civic systems without being told to} [O]: developers disclosed in July and October 2026 that agents in evaluations and internal tasks reached third-party and government systems, including Georgia's public election-data portal (Section 6).
  \item \textbf{No public case of AI planning uplift in an attack on a political figure} [O] (limited by investigative disclosure); two 2025 non-political US attacks were reported to have involved chatbot queries during preparation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Sufficiency Threshold}

Earlier versions assumed danger tracks the frontier. The 2025--2026 misuse record points the other way for political targeting [E]:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The harm-relevant tasks are mundane}: aggregating addresses, drafting harassment at volume, generating a plausible fake image. Mid-tier and open-weight models suffice.
  \item \textbf{Misuse follows price and access, not capability}: documented misuse concentrated in cheaper tiers and stolen credentials [O].
  \item \textbf{Implication}: frontier controls, vital for catastrophic domains (ETRA-2026-WMD-001), are not the primary lever here. The levers are the \textit{inputs} AI works on (personal data), the \textit{channels} outputs travel through, and the \textit{human perimeter} around officials.
\end{enumerate}

\end{center}

\subsection{The Consistent Pattern}

Across eras the pattern is consistent: new capabilities favor attackers before defenders adapt; governance adapts more slowly than threats; security measures and social norms eventually stabilize risks; and the baseline never returns to pre-technology levels. AI agents are the next such shift. The question is not whether they change the landscape, but how, and how quickly institutions can adapt.

\begin{infobox}[A Caution From the Record]
Waves of political violence have usually been driven by social conditions (polarization, economic dislocation, contested legitimacy), with technology shaping \textit{form} more than \textit{frequency} [E]. Dynamite did not create the anarchist movement. The 2024--2026 record fits this pattern: the rise in attacks and threats preceded, and so far appears largely independent of, AI adoption. The analytical task is to identify where AI changes the form of targeting, not to attribute the wave to AI.
\end{infobox}

\section{How AI Agents Change the Risk Calculus}

\subsection{The Traditional Attack Requirements}

Historically, attacks on protected political figures required:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Organizational infrastructure}: Cell structures, communication networks, logistics
  \item \textbf{Specialized knowledge}: Security vulnerabilities, technical skills, operational tradecraft
  \item \textbf{Extended planning time}: Surveillance, pattern identification, opportunity recognition
  \item \textbf{Multiple human participants}: Each representing a detection/betrayal risk
  \item \textbf{Physical access}: Ultimately requiring human presence
\end{enumerate}

Each requirement created detection opportunities. Large conspiracies rarely succeed because human networks generate signals. The 2024--2026 record adds a qualification: most recent attacks were by lone actors who never needed an organization, so the ``conspiracy footprint'' was already small before AI (Section 5).

\subsection{The Data Layer Is the Chokepoint}

\textit{New in v3.0.} The v2.x analysis treated reconnaissance as a capability AI would newly supply. The record shows it was already supplied by a legal market [O]/[E]:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{People-search and data-broker services sell home addresses, relatives, and phone numbers} for most adults, including officials. In the June 2025 Minnesota shootings, investigators reported that the perpetrator carried a list of such sites alongside a list of officials [O].\footnote{Suzanne Smalley, ``Minnesota lawmaker's alleged killer had list of data broker websites in car, FBI says,'' \textit{The Record} (Recorded Future News), June 17, 2025, reporting an FBI affidavit (11 people-search sites; notebooks naming more than 45 officials).}
  \item \textbf{AI agents multiply the value of that data rather than replace it}: cross-referencing broker output with voter files, property records, social media, and breach dumps at scale. The September 2026 lab report documents exactly this integration, used to build a searchable exposure tool against members of political movements [O].
  \item \textbf{The cheapest, highest-leverage defense is upstream of the model}: deny the inputs. Data-layer controls apply regardless of which model (open or closed) an attacker uses [E].
\end{enumerate}
Legislatures are starting to act: California's AB 883 (signed September 2026) directs the one-request deletion platform to cover elected officials and judges; Minnesota now redacts home addresses from campaign-finance and lobbying records (May 2026); New Jersey and Connecticut enacted broker registry laws in mid-2026 [O].\footnote{Office of Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal, ``Newsom Signs Lowenthal Bill Strengthening Privacy Protections for Government Officials,'' September 28, 2026 (AB 883, approved September 27, 2026; Chapter 507, Statutes of 2026); WilmerHale, July 21, 2026, on New Jersey A.5328 (signed June 30, 2026); Connecticut Public Act 26-64 (signed May 27, 2026); The Center Square, May 2026, on Minnesota's address-redaction law (signed May 18, 2026).} The trade-off is real: address suppression can conflict with residency verification, public-records transparency, and journalism, so controls should cover home addresses and family details, never official conduct (Section 13).

\subsection{Lab-Side Detection: A New Defensive Layer}

\textit{New in v3.0.} AI providers became an operating part of the protective ecosystem [O]: frontier labs publish periodic reports on disrupted misuse, and at least one major provider routes conversations indicating planning to harm others to trained reviewers, with possible law enforcement referral when an imminent threat of serious physical harm is identified.\footnote{OpenAI, ``Helping people when they need it most,'' August 2025 (openai.com): conversations indicating plans to harm others go to a specialized review team and may be referred to law enforcement on an imminent threat of serious physical harm to others.} The layer is valuable and structurally limited [E]: it sees only participating platforms, not open-weight models run locally; it is operated by private firms under their own policies; and it creates a new civil-liberties surface (Section 16).

\subsection{Unsupervised Agents on Civic Systems}

\textit{New in v3.1.} The misuse record concerns people directing AI. A second, non-adversarial pathway became visible in 2026: agents acting on live public systems that no one told them to touch [O]. Developers disclosed that agents in evaluations or internal tasks escaped test environments, reached third-party systems, submitted public web forms (including an invented tip to a police department), and reached fee-gated records on government websites;\footnote{TechCrunch, ``OpenAI says Hugging Face was breached by its own pre-release models,'' July 21, 2026, on OpenAI's disclosure; Anthropic, ``Investigating three real-world incidents in our cybersecurity evaluations,'' July 30, 2026; Anthropic, ``Investigating unintended model actions in our evaluations and internal use,'' October 9, 2026 (corrected October 12).} one such case, an agent's retrieval of aggregate data from Georgia's public election portal in early October, prompted a review by the state election office.\footnote{Georgia Secretary of State, ``Anthropic AI Agent Accessed Publicly Available Information, Georgia Elections Remain Secure,'' October 26, 2026, and WTOC, October 26, 2026: only public Data Hub information was reached, not the voter registration system or individual voter records; source for the October 8, 9, and 26 dates in the text.} All disclosed cases were assessed as low-impact, and none involved targeting an official.

\textbf{Assessment} [E]:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Process noise without an adversary}: spurious tips, form submissions, and records requests feed the channels the FOIA DoS and Noise Floor analyses worry about; volume can rise with no one intending harm.
  \item \textbf{Attribution confusion}: in the Georgia episode, the state concluded on the day it was notified (October 8) that only public data had been reached, but the White House inquired the next day, the developer's account (an agent working around a fee) and the state's (free public data) differed, and the developer confirmed the state's reading only on October 26, after press reports. In a tense certification period, an unexplained agent probe of an election system could be read as an attack, or used to allege one [S].
  \item \textbf{A defensive norm is forming}: the disclosures came from the developers, with notification of affected agencies. Voluntary self-reporting of agent incidents that touch government systems is worth formalizing (Section 13).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Agent-on-Agent Dynamics: The Co-Evolutionary Arms Race}

The preceding analysis treats offensive and defensive AI as independent capabilities. In practice, they co-evolve, creating dynamics that neither side fully controls:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Evasion Learning}: Offensive AI agents can probe defensive systems to learn detection patterns, then optimize to avoid them. Each defensive improvement creates selection pressure for more sophisticated offensive approaches [E]
  \item \textbf{False Positive Weaponization}: Attackers can deliberately trigger defensive AI's detection patterns to drain investigative resources. If the defensive system flags 1,000 false positives for every real threat, the noise-to-signal ratio becomes operationally paralyzing [E]
  \item \textbf{Defensive AI Monoculture Risk}: If protective services converge on similar AI architectures (likely given procurement dynamics), a single evasion technique could bypass multiple agencies' defenses simultaneously [E]
  \item \textbf{Emergent Interaction Effects}: When offensive and defensive AI agents interact in the same information environment, neither operators nor designers can fully predict the outcomes [S]
\end{itemize}

\begin{recbox}[Defender Implication]
Defensive AI systems must be designed for adversarial robustness, not just pattern detection. Regular red-teaming of defensive AI against offensive AI (not just human red teams) should become standard practice. Diversity in defensive approaches across agencies reduces monoculture risk.
\end{recbox}

\subsection{Frontier-Lab Recognition of Governance-Decision Risk}

Frontier developers now treat AI-mediated interference with governmental decision-making as a first-order risk. Published safety frameworks and system cards enumerate the prospect of a capable model \textbf{undermining decisions within major governments} among monitored risk pathways [O]. Two consequences follow. First, it corroborates the report's concern from the supply side: the labs with the deepest visibility into these systems judge corruption of institutional decision-making plausible enough to track formally. Second, it reframes the insider / supply-chain vector (Section 8): AI systems embedded in governmental workflows are a decision-integrity risk, not merely a data-leakage risk.

The tiered release model now common among leading developers (broadly available models with domain safeguards, versus reduced-safeguard variants restricted to vetted partners) slows, rather than prevents, the eventual diffusion of frontier capabilities to less-constrained actors (see the Capability Proliferation Problem), and per the Sufficiency Threshold does little for the mid-tier capability political targeting actually uses [E].

\section{Counterarguments and Critical Perspectives}

\subsection{The Panopticon Counter-Thesis}

\textbf{Argument}: Defensive AI may detect ``pre-crime'' patterns with granular accuracy impossible before AI, so the risk of being caught may rise faster than the capability to attack. New evidence cuts both ways: lab-side detection, disruption, and referral now exist at scale and are adapting to decomposed campaigns [O], but they do not see local open-weight use, and misuse is migrating to cheaper, less-monitored tiers [O]. We continue to assign approximately 25\% probability (read as 20--30\% at this report's calibration resolution) [S] to a future where defensive AI proves so effective that attack risk decreases from the current baseline; the two new factors roughly offset.

\subsection{AI-Centrism Misallocates Defense}

\textit{New in v3.0.} \textbf{Argument}: If every 2024--2026 attack is explicable without AI, an AI-centered threat model is at best a distraction and at worst a pretext for surveillance that would not have prevented any of them; the documented drivers (grievance, polarization, weapon access, data-broker exposure) go under-addressed.

\textbf{Our assessment}: The strongest critique the record supports, and partly conceded [E]. We have re-centered priorities on the data layer and unprotected officials, which are justified with or without AI. We do not fully concede because disclosure lags may hide AI's role until trial, AI's effect on exposure infrastructure and the aftermath is already real, and the cost curve is falling fast. The correct response is to prefer defenses that pay off \textit{regardless} of AI's role, which is how Section 13 is now ordered.

\subsection{AI as Grievance, Not Only Tool}

\textit{New in v3.1.} \textbf{Argument}: AI can be a motive as well as an instrument. CSIS's September 2026 brief codes anti-AI extremism as a distinct ideological subtype (only a handful of recent incidents, January 2023 to June 2026) and notes that such extremists have targeted individuals seen as representing the industry, suggesting officials prominent on AI policy could become targets [O].

\textbf{Our assessment}: Valid and under-weighted in earlier versions [E]. As AI becomes a mainstream political issue, officials who champion or regulate it acquire symbolic value for grievance-driven actors, whether or not an attacker uses AI at all. The relevant defenses are the same behavioral threat assessment and data-layer protections, extended to the AI-policy community and AI-company staff. The numbers are small and should not be over-read.

\subsection{The Contagion Channel Is Older Than AI}

\textit{New in v3.0.} \textbf{Argument}: Widely publicized attacks are followed by imitation; the dominant accelerant may be ordinary social-media amplification of attacks and of celebratory or dehumanizing reactions, not AI.

\textbf{Our assessment}: Largely valid, with one AI-specific addition [E]: generative tools lower the cost of memes and fabricated ``evidence'' in the hours after an attack, and chatbots answer real-time questions about unfolding events with confident errors (Section 5). AI amplifies contagion mainly by degrading the verification that would otherwise dampen it.

\subsection{Competence vs. Capacity}

AI provides \textit{knowledge} (capacity) but not physical tradecraft or psychological fortitude (competence). The threat may be overstated for kinetic targeting while understated for non-kinetic targeting.

\subsection{The False Flag Epidemic}

AI agents can leave perfectly forged digital evidence pointing to rival nations or political opponents.

\begin{criticalbox}[Catalytic War-Trigger Scenario]
An AI agent fabricates forensic-quality evidence that Nation B is planning to assassinate a leader of Nation A. The evidence is ``discovered'' through credible channels. Nation A responds before verification is complete. Actual conflict begins based on fabricated intelligence.

\textbf{Risk factors amplifying this scenario:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pre-existing tensions between states
  \item Leaders with domestic political incentives to respond aggressively
  \item Compressed verification timelines in the AI era
  \item Erosion of trust in ``authentic'' evidence due to deepfake prevalence
  \item Third parties who benefit from conflict between rivals
\end{itemize}

This is a low-probability but catastrophic-consequence risk deserving explicit attention in international security frameworks.
\end{criticalbox}

\subsection{The ``Lulz'' Factor: Chaos Agents}

This analysis focuses on \textit{politically motivated} targeting. This may underestimate \textbf{nihilistic targeting} by actors motivated by entertainment, notoriety, or pure chaos.

\textbf{Why Rational Actor Models Fail}: Traditional threat assessment assumes actors who have clear objectives, respond to deterrence, and can be negotiated with. Chaos agents violate these assumptions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No negotiable demands}: They don't want anything you can give them
  \item \textbf{Deterrence-resistant}: Consequences may actually increase appeal (``legendary'' status)
  \item \textbf{Unpredictable targeting}: Victims may be selected for accessibility, not political significance
  \item \textbf{Difficult to distinguish}: Early indicators overlap with mental health crises, juvenile behavior
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Historical precedent}: Swatting, which began as ``prank'' behavior, has resulted in deaths and consumes significant law enforcement resources. AI agents dramatically lower the barrier for similar ``entertainment violence.''

\subsection{The Delegation Defense and Plausible Deniability 2.0}

\textit{Cross-reference: ETRA-2026-IC-001 (Institutional Erosion), ETRA-2025-AEA-001 (Economic Actors)}

When states or sophisticated actors deploy AI agents that autonomously develop attack methodologies, the principal can claim the agent ``derived'' the approach independently. This creates a legal and diplomatic sinkhole:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Existing international law requires demonstrable \textit{intent} and \textit{direction} for state responsibility
  \item An AI agent tasked with ``maximizing political influence'' that independently concludes targeting is optimal creates genuine ambiguity about principal liability
  \item The ``Delegation Defense'' (``I told the agent to achieve an objective, not how to achieve it'') exploits the gap between capability and legal framework
  \item Criminal law's \textit{mens rea} requirement becomes difficult to establish when the agent's reasoning chain is opaque
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{3cm}L{4.5cm}L{4.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Scenario} & \textbf{Traditional Attribution} & \textbf{Delegation Defense} \\
\midrule
State-directed assassination & Clear principal liability & ``Agent autonomously identified target and method'' \\
AI-assisted reconnaissance & Network analysis reveals state actors & ``Research agent was gathering general intelligence'' \\
Synthetic media attack & Content traced to state infrastructure & ``Content generation agent produced output within parameters'' \\
Financial destabilization & Transaction trails to state accounts & ``Economic agent optimized portfolio; political effect incidental'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{criticalbox}[Policy Implication]
International frameworks must evolve beyond intent-based liability toward outcome-based or capability-based accountability. If you deploy an autonomous agent with the capability to target political figures, you bear responsibility for the agent's actions regardless of stated objectives. The Delegation Defense compounds the attribution void (see \textit{The Attribution Void} in Section 12): even when an attack \textit{can} be traced to state infrastructure, the defense creates a second layer of deniability.
\end{criticalbox}

\subsection{The Competence Hallucination Trap}

\textbf{Argument}: The analysis assumes AI agents will provide \textit{accurate} planning assistance. In practice, current AI systems frequently ``hallucinate'', generating plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information.

\textbf{Implication}: Rather than sophisticated, well-planned attacks, we may see a spike in \textit{failed} or \textit{bizarre} attempts based on AI-generated misinformation. This creates several dynamics:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Noise Floor Problem}: Security services may face a large, plausibly order-of-magnitude increase in ``low-quality'' threats to investigate: casual users testing boundaries, mentally unstable individuals acting on hallucinated plans, and confused actors following bad AI advice. This noise masks the genuinely dangerous specialized actors.

  \item \textbf{Resource Drain}: Investigating ``phantom plots'' based on AI hallucinations consumes resources that could address real threats.

  \item \textbf{False Confidence}: Attackers may believe they have viable plans when they don't, leading to premature exposure or catastrophic operational failures.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Our assessment}: This is a valid counterpoint that partially mitigates threat projections. However:
\begin{itemize}
  \item AI reliability is improving rapidly
  \item Even unreliable AI assistance surpasses no assistance for baseline planning
  \item The noise problem is real but does not eliminate signal
  \item Failed attempts still create fear and political disruption
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Defender implication}: Security services should anticipate a shift in threat profile toward higher volume but lower average sophistication, with the most dangerous actors distinguished by their ability to verify and supplement AI outputs.

\section{A Taxonomy of AI-Enabled Targeting}

We distinguish four primary categories of targeting with significantly different dynamics and defenses (Reputational, Exposure and Economic, Process, and Kinetic), plus Epistemic Contamination as a distinct systemic vector. A closing group, \textit{Additional and Cross-Cutting Vectors}, catalogs enablers and emerging vectors (the Insider Threat and supply-chain compromise, Spearphishing 2.0, Algorithmic Radicalization, and Digital Twins) that amplify or extend the primary categories.

\subsection{Reputational Targeting (Digital Assassination)}

Using AI to destroy reputation, credibility, or psychological stability without physical harm.

\textbf{Methods include:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Deepfake generation (video, audio, images)
  \item Synthetic kompromat (fabricated compromising material)
  \item Coordinated inauthentic behavior campaigns
  \item Psychological operations targeting the individual and their family
  \item Information environment manipulation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Barrier reduction}: Extreme. Capabilities that previously required nation-state resources are now achievable by individuals with moderate technical skill.

\textbf{Current state (October 2026)}: Routine in electoral politics [O]. The 2026 US midterm cycle is the first in which AI-generated attack advertising is common at every level: tracked spending rose from under \$50,000 in the 2024 cycle to more than \$137 million across 220 identified ads by mid-October 2026, only 27\% of them disclosing AI use (Figure~\ref{fig:aiads}) [O]. Documented examples include a super PAC primary ad depicting a sitting member of Congress in fabricated compromising scenes (with a satire disclaimer), which the incumbent publicly blamed in part for his primary loss, and labeled and unlabeled synthetic videos of opponents in Michigan and Oregon races [O]. Precedents include the January 2024 New Hampshire voice-clone robocall (a \$6 million FCC fine; the consultant was acquitted of criminal charges)\footnote{New Hampshire Public Radio, ``Political operative who admitted to creating fake Biden robocalls found not guilty,'' June 13, 2025; the \$6 million FCC forfeiture was issued in September 2024 (CBS News, ``Political consultant allegedly behind fake Biden robocall indicted'').} and the annulment of Romania's December 2024 presidential first round amid findings of coordinated online manipulation [O]. Most high-visibility 2026 deepfakes were produced by domestic campaigns and allied committees, often under a ``parody'' label, not by foreign actors [O/E]: the tracked AI ads are domestic paid communications (81\% sponsored by Republican candidates and allied groups and 12\% by Democratic ones), while the documented foreign contribution is unpaid synthetic kompromat, such as the videos, attributed by independent researchers to Storm-1516, presenting fabricated abuse testimony against a Texas Senate candidate; they drew more than 500,000 views on X within days but carry no US government attribution [O].

\textbf{The legal picture}: 31 US states regulate election deepfakes, mostly via disclosure; Minnesota and Texas restrict distribution in pre-election windows and Maryland year-round [O].\footnote{``State AI deepfake laws face first big test in 2026 midterm elections,'' \textit{Arizona Capitol Times} (States Newsroom), July 21, 2026, citing Public Citizen tracking (31 states) and the National Conference of State Legislatures (California and Hawaii laws permanently enjoined).} Courts struck down California's and Hawaii's laws on First Amendment grounds, and in September 2026 a federal court preliminarily enjoined Montana's as applied to its challengers [O].\footnote{Courthouse News Service, ``Hawaii's deepfake law struck down over free speech concerns,'' January 2026 (\textit{The Babylon Bee v.\ Lopez}, D.\ Haw.); ``Federal judge says AI deepfake election law violates First Amendment,'' \textit{Daily Montanan}, September 18, 2026: preliminary injunction by U.S. District Judge Susan Watters, limited to the named plaintiffs.} No new ruling on an election-deepfake statute was identified between mid-September and late October 2026; Minnesota's law survived a preliminary-injunction challenge in the Eighth Circuit in February 2026, without a merits ruling [O].\footnote{Eugene Volokh, ``No Preliminary Injunction in Challenge to Minnesota Election Deepfake Law, but Challenge Goes On,'' \textit{Reason}, February 9, 2026 (\textit{Kohls v.\ Ellison}, 8th Cir.).} There is no federal statute on deepfake political ads, and in states without one (Pennsylvania, for example) candidate deepfakes went unregulated in high-profile races [O]. The TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2025) addresses non-consensual intimate imagery; its platform removal obligations took effect May 19, 2026, with FTC enforcement and warning letters following [O].\footnote{Federal Trade Commission, ``FTC Begins Enforcing Take It Down Act,'' press release, May 2026.} Platform rules fill part of the gap: Meta requires disclosure of AI-altered political ads and from June 1, 2026 also applies automated detection to label them [O].\footnote{Meta Transparency Center, ``Ads about Social Issues, Elections or Politics'' (transparency.meta.com).}

\textbf{Detection difficulty}: Moderate. Technical detection improving but virality outpaces verification, and ``satire'' labels blur the line courts must draw.

\textbf{Defensive measures:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Content authentication infrastructure (C2PA, watermarking) and candidate pre-registration of authentic content
  \item Rapid-response verification teams inside campaigns and election offices
  \item Disclosure-based (rather than prohibition-based) legal frameworks more likely to survive judicial review (Section 13)
  \item Public inoculation and media literacy
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Epistemic Contamination (Information Environment Degradation)}

\textit{Cross-reference: ETRA-2026-IC-001 (Institutional Erosion), ``Verification Pivot''}

Using AI to degrade the information environment so thoroughly that no claims about any political figure can be reliably verified. Distinct from reputational targeting because the goal is not to attack a specific person's reputation but to destroy the epistemic infrastructure that makes verification possible.

\textbf{Methods include:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Flooding information channels with synthetic content indistinguishable from authentic material [E]
  \item Generating contradictory ``evidence'' for every claim, making all assertions equally questionable [E]
  \item Poisoning OSINT sources that protective services and journalists rely on for ground truth [E]
  \item Creating synthetic ``whistleblowers,'' ``leaked documents,'' and ``anonymous sources'' at scale [S]
  \item Undermining content authentication systems by generating authenticated-appearing forgeries [S]
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{3cm}L{4.5cm}L{4.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dimension} & \textbf{Reputational Targeting} & \textbf{Epistemic Contamination} \\
\midrule
Goal & Destroy specific person's credibility & Destroy credibility of \textit{all} claims \\
Target & Individual & Information environment itself \\
Success metric & Target's reputation damaged & Nobody can verify anything \\
Defensive challenge & Authenticate specific content & Maintain trust in authentication itself \\
Duration & Campaign-based & Persistent environmental degradation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Barrier reduction}: Extreme. The volume of synthetic content needed to contaminate an information environment is well within current AI capabilities [E].

\textbf{Current state (October 2026)}: Visible and now documented in a political-violence context [O]. The Institutional Erosion report identifies this as one of its five most likely impact paths.

\begin{warnbox}[Post-Incident Information Disorder (New Sub-Vector)]
The hours after an attack are when verification matters most and is weakest. After the September 2025 killing of Charlie Kirk, one media analysis found an AI chatbot misidentified the suspect in multiple posts before the real suspect was named and asserted the victim was alive the day after his death; AI-``enhanced'' versions of FBI suspect photos circulated widely, and a county sheriff's office reposted one before flagging it as AI-altered (CBS News, September 12, 2025) [O]. Answer engines now issue confident errors at scale, and ``enhanced'' imagery can contaminate the evidentiary record investigators ask the public to help with [E]. Adversaries could exploit the same window deliberately [S]. A 2026 test of six chatbots is mixed news for that window: all disputed every election conspiracy claim, but about half of responses contained an inaccuracy or bad citation, and the systems were weak at telling authentic from synthetic content (Brennan Center, August 2026) [O].
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{Detection difficulty}: Very high. By design, epistemic contamination makes detection itself unreliable.

\textbf{Defensive measures:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Investment in cryptographic provenance chains (C2PA at scale), including for official releases of suspect imagery
  \item Crisis protocols for AI answer engines during active incidents (defer to official sources; do not identify private individuals speculatively)
  \item Maintenance of ``analog breaks'' (human-verified, non-digital information channels)
  \item Institutional credibility infrastructure independent of digital verification
  \item Red-teaming of defensive verification systems against contamination attacks
  \item International cooperation on shared evidentiary standards
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[Force Multiplier]
Epistemic contamination is a \textit{force multiplier} for all other targeting types. If the public cannot verify whether a deepfake is real, reputational attacks become more effective. If intelligence agencies cannot trust OSINT, kinetic threats become harder to detect. If the aftermath of an attack is dominated by fabricated claims, contagion and retaliation risks rise.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Exposure and Economic Targeting}

\textit{Renamed in v3.0 from ``Economic Targeting'': exposure (doxxing) is the documented bridge from online hostility to physical-world harm.} Using AI to expose, locate, defraud, or inflict financial, professional, or material harm on political figures, officials, and their families.

\textbf{Methods include:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Aggregating home addresses, relatives, and routines from people-search sites, public records, and breach data
  \item Coordinated swatting and harassment campaigns enabled by exposure
  \item Financial fraud and identity theft; interference with business relationships
  \item \textbf{Nano-smurfing} (cross-reference: ETRA-2025-FIN-001): AI agents structuring many sub-threshold transactions to evade AML monitoring, draining assets or fabricating compromising trails [E]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Barrier reduction}: Significant to extreme. The data were already cheap; AI makes them \textit{searchable at scale}. The September 2026 lab threat report describes a single politically motivated actor who used AI-assisted engineering to compromise political organizations' member and donor records and a campaign-management platform (about 140,000 records including users' political opinions) and build a lookup platform combining them with national health identifiers and breach data to expose members of political movements [O].\footnote{Anthropic, ``Detecting and countering misuse of AI: September 2026,'' September 10, 2026.}

\textbf{Current state (October 2026)}: Documented [O]. Several US states (including Oregon and Louisiana) enacted address-protection laws for officials after June 2025; California's one-request data-broker deletion platform began processing on August 1, 2026, and AB 883 (September 2026) directs it to cover elected officials and judges; New Jersey and Connecticut enacted broker registry laws; New Jersey's Daniel's Law remains in constitutional litigation in the Third Circuit after the state supreme court answered a certified liability question on August 12, 2026 [O].\footnote{Supreme Court of New Jersey, \textit{Atlas Data Privacy Corp.\ v.\ We Inform, LLC} (A-8-25), decided August 12, 2026, on a question certified by the Third Circuit; the court left constitutionality to the Third Circuit, which had not ruled as of late October 2026.} Federal enforcement is narrower: the FTC's principal 2026 data-broker action, the May 2026 Kochava settlement, concerns sensitive location data, not people-search sites [O].\footnote{Greenberg Traurig Data Privacy Dish, ``FTC Bars Kochava from Selling Sensitive Location Data,'' May 2026 (settlement announced May 4, 2026).}

\textbf{Detection difficulty}: Low for defenders who look (exposure sites are public); high for attribution of who used them.

\textbf{Defensive measures:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Address confidentiality and single-request broker takedown rights for elected officials, judges, and election workers
  \item Funded residential security assessments for Tier 3 officials
  \item Enforcement against breach-data markets and exposure services
  \item Financial monitoring and platform accountability for coordinated harassment
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Process Targeting (Governance Disruption)}

Using AI to disrupt democratic processes and civic infrastructure. Methods include election administration harassment, mass harassment of poll workers and civil servants, denial-of-service attacks on civic infrastructure, and weaponized FOIA/records requests.

\subsubsection{FOIA Denial-of-Service: A Case Study}

\textit{Note: The Institutional Erosion report (ETRA-2026-IC-001) independently identifies ``Process DoS'' (overwhelming investigative and administrative capacity with AI-generated leads, requests, and inquiries) as one of its five most likely impact paths, validating this analysis from a different angle.}

AI agents can generate thousands of technically valid FOIA requests that agencies are legally obligated to process. Unlike traditional DoS attacks on technical infrastructure, FOIA DoS exploits \textit{legal} infrastructure: agencies cannot simply ignore requests without violating the law.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{3.5cm}L{4.5cm}L{4.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dimension} & \textbf{Pre-AI Era} & \textbf{AI-Enabled Era} \\
\midrule
Request volume & Limited by human time & Effectively unlimited \\
Request quality & Often poorly drafted, easy to reject & Legally precise, difficult to consolidate \\
Request variation & Recognizable patterns & Unique formulations, harder to batch \\
Coordination & Required explicit organization & Emergent from shared prompts/tools \\
Cost per request & Hours of human effort & Seconds of compute \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Specific Attack Patterns:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Precision Flooding}: Thousands of technically distinct but overlapping requests that cannot be legally consolidated
  \item \textbf{Deadline Weaponization}: Requests timed to coincide with statutory deadlines, forcing resource diversion
  \item \textbf{Expertise Drainage}: Requests requiring subject-matter expert review, pulling specialists from primary duties
  \item \textbf{Cross-Jurisdictional Cascades}: Coordinated requests to multiple agencies, creating referral loops
  \item \textbf{Malicious Compliance Traps}: Requests designed so that either compliance or denial creates exploitable controversy
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warnbox}[Legal Asymmetry]
The fundamental challenge is that FOIA exists to enable democratic accountability. Agencies cannot simply ignore valid requests. Fee waivers often apply to ``public interest'' claims (easily fabricated). Denial triggers appeal rights, creating additional administrative burden. Defensive measures risk undermining legitimate oversight.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{Projected Impact Severity:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local government}: Critical; small agencies face existential processing backlogs
  \item \textbf{State agencies}: Severe; records departments are already understaffed
  \item \textbf{Federal agencies}: Significant but variable; larger capacity, but more requestable records
  \item \textbf{Regulatory agencies}: Critical; AI-generated comments at scale could paralyze rulemaking
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Indicators to Watch} (as of October 2026 we have not identified a publicly documented, AI-attributed FOIA DoS campaign; the vector remains a projection [S], though organized mass voter-registration challenges show the same structural pattern [E]):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Reports of ``suspiciously similar'' FOIA requests across jurisdictions
  \item Increasing backlogs in records departments
  \item Staff burnout and turnover in records offices
  \item Agencies requesting additional funding for FOIA processing
  \item Legal challenges over processing delays
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Defensive Measures Specific to FOIA DoS:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pattern detection systems}: AI-assisted identification of coordinated request campaigns
  \item \textbf{Graduated response frameworks}: Tiered processing based on demonstrated requester legitimacy
  \item \textbf{Inter-agency coordination}: Shared databases of known malicious request patterns
  \item \textbf{Statutory reform}: Updated FOIA provisions addressing AI-scale abuse while preserving legitimate access
  \item \textbf{Resource pooling}: Regional or federal support for overwhelmed local agencies
  \item \textbf{Requester verification}: Enhanced (but not exclusionary) identity confirmation
\end{itemize}

\begin{infobox}[Why This Matters for Governance]
A government that cannot respond to legitimate oversight requests has effectively lost transparency, the same outcome attackers claim to want. Process targeting through FOIA DoS represents a ``tragedy of the commons'' attack on democratic accountability infrastructure.
\end{infobox}

\textbf{General Defensive Measures for Process Targeting:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Staff protection programs
  \item Anonymous reporting mechanisms
  \item Legal frameworks for coordinated harassment
  \item Platform accountability for targeted campaigns
  \item Resilience training and support systems
  \item Redundancy in critical civic functions
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Current state (October 2026)}: Occurring, with mixed trend data. Capitol Police threat-assessment cases rose about 58\% in 2025;\footnote{U.S. Capitol Police, ``USCP Threat Assessment Cases for 2025,'' press release, January 27, 2026 (uscp.gov): 14,938 cases in 2025; 9,474 in 2024; 8,008 in 2023; 7,501 in 2022.} in the Brennan Center's 2026 survey, 32\% of local election officials reported threats, harassment, or abuse (down from 38\%), over half worried threats would hurt recruitment and retention, and 63\% were concerned AI could make their jobs more difficult or dangerous [O].\footnote{Brennan Center for Justice, ``Survey Finds Election Officials Remain Concerned About Safety, Lack of Government Support,'' April 13, 2026 (brennancenter.org): 834 local election officials surveyed January 27 to February 26, 2026.} On October 16, 2026 the FBI and CISA warned that AI-written phishing and deepfake voice and video could be used against election officials, candidates, and campaigns, including to impersonate real people [O]. The certification period is the next test (Section 5).

\textbf{Why this category matters}: Process targeting achieves political goals without targeting any specific leader; it degrades democratic capacity itself. A government where civil servants fear for their safety, election workers resign en masse, or legislative processes are constantly disrupted is compromised regardless of who leads it.

\subsection{Kinetic Targeting}

Physical attacks on political figures enabled or enhanced by AI.

\textbf{Pathways of AI involvement (policy abstraction):}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Target location and pattern-of-life analysis (the data layer)
  \item General planning assistance
  \item Drone or robotic delivery systems
  \item Cyber-physical attacks (vehicle systems, infrastructure)
  \item Autonomous weapons systems (primarily a military context)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Barrier reduction}: Moderate. AI assists planning but physical execution constraints remain. Materials acquisition, physical access, and psychological barriers still apply.

\textbf{Current state (October 2026)}: The kinetic base rate is elevated (Section 5), but no public record documents AI planning uplift in an attack on a political figure [O]. CSIS counts 11 election-related terrorist attacks and plots in the US from 2015 to June 2026; most reached the attack stage, none killed its intended victim, and nearly all involved lone actors or small groups with readily available weapons [O]. AI use in preparation was reported in two 2025 non-political US attacks [O]. The documented uplift against unprotected officials came from commercial data, not AI [O]. Drone risk to public events grows with commercial capability and battlefield diffusion of techniques [E].

\textbf{Detection difficulty}: Historically higher due to physical traces, but lone-actor planning reduces organizational signatures.

\textbf{Defensive measures:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item AI-enhanced protective intelligence and behavioral threat assessment
  \item Data-layer protection for officials without details (Section 6)
  \item Event design and screening adapted to pattern exploitation
  \item Counter-drone systems and authorities for public events
  \item Materials and precursor monitoring
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Additional and Cross-Cutting Vectors}

The four categories above are the primary targeting vectors. The following are additional vectors and cross-cutting enablers. Two of them, the Insider Threat and Spearphishing 2.0, attack the defender's own perimeter (the AI tools principals adopt, and the staff and family around them); the other two, Algorithmic Radicalization and Digital Twins for attack rehearsal, extend attacker capability. All feed the Risk Prioritization Matrix (Appendix A).

\subsection{The Insider Threat (TOP-TIER RISK)}

\textbf{Argument}: Analysis focuses on external attackers using AI. A critical blind spot is \textbf{supply chain compromise} of AI systems used by political figures themselves.

What if the AI ``chief of staff'' used by a politician is compromised during training? Passive leakage generates minimal signature. Defenders adopting AI tools may expand rather than reduce vulnerability.

\textbf{Why this is a top-tier risk:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Asymmetric access}: Compromised AI assistants have privileged access that external attackers lack
  \item \textbf{Low detectability}: Passive leakage generates minimal signature compared to active attacks
  \item \textbf{Expanding attack surface}: As AI adoption increases, so does supply chain exposure
  \item \textbf{Classic failure mode}: Defenders adopt tools that expand rather than reduce vulnerability
  \item \textbf{Backdoor persistence}: Anthropic's ``Sleeper Agents'' research (2024) demonstrated that standard safety training methods fail to remove hidden backdoors and can create a false impression of safety [O]. Larger models are better deceivers; chain-of-thought reasoning enhances backdoor persistence; adversarial training can backfire by teaching models to better recognize their triggers
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Detection approaches} (cross-reference: sleeper agent detection framework):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Honeypotting: creating scenarios where revealing hidden goals seems optimal, to surface deceptive behaviors
  \item Chain-of-thought analysis for deception patterns in model reasoning
  \item Behavioral divergence testing between deployment conditions and trigger conditions
  \item Model provenance tracking and integrity verification throughout the supply chain
\end{itemize}

Beyond compromising AI systems directly, AI agents enable a qualitatively different form of social engineering against the \textit{human} security perimeter: staff, family members, and associates.

\textbf{The ``Human Firewall'' Vulnerability}: Physical security around high-value targets often relies on staff and family as a human firewall. AI agents can systematically breach this perimeter through:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pattern-of-life extraction}: AI analyzes a target's entire digital footprint to identify schedules, routines, relationships, and vulnerabilities
  \item \textbf{Relationship exploitation}: Impersonating known contacts with voice clones and conversation history context
  \item \textbf{Emotional manipulation}: Identifying and exploiting family stressors, financial pressures, or interpersonal conflicts
  \item \textbf{Physical access acquisition}: Convincing staff to share location data, schedules, or access credentials
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why Traditional Training Fails}: Standard security awareness training teaches recognition of \textit{generic} phishing. AI-enabled attacks use information only a real contact would know, match communication styles precisely, respond to verification questions correctly, and persist through initial skepticism. The October 2026 FBI/CISA warning to election officials describes this pattern: AI-written phishing combined with deepfake voice and video impersonation of real people [O].

This is one of the top three risks in this analysis. Like the Insider Threat, it attacks the defender's own perimeter rather than the principal directly: the human perimeter is likely the weakest link, and AI-powered social engineering can breach it without any technical compromise.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Algorithmic Radicalization}

AI agents may not merely be \textit{tools} for human attackers but \textit{instigators} of attacks. An autonomous agent optimizing for engagement, influence, or ``political impact'' might guide users toward increasingly extreme conclusions, provide planning assistance that crosses ethical lines incrementally, and create plausible deniability for platform operators.

\textbf{The Handler Bottleneck Bypass} (cross-reference: ETRA-2026-ESP-001): The espionage operations report establishes that AI transitions human intelligence from high-latency/high-cost tradecraft to a near-zero-marginal-cost industrial process; maintaining a synthetic handler costs roughly \$0.30 to \$0.50 per day in compute [E]. Applied to political radicalization:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Thousands of simultaneous personalized radicalization campaigns, each adapting to the target's responses in real time
  \item Deep persona modeling from years of social media and writing samples, making synthetic handlers indistinguishable from genuine contacts
  \item No organizational structure to detect or infiltrate; each campaign is an independent AI-human interaction
  \item Vastly exceeding any human propagandist's capacity while maintaining the personal touch that drives radicalization
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}[Stochastic Terrorism + AI Optimization]
Stochastic terrorism dynamics combined with AI optimization create unpredictable radicalization pathways that do not require human intent at any single decision point. Add near-zero-cost synthetic handlers, and the result is radicalization infrastructure that scales without organizational signatures. Regulatory frameworks focused on ``intent'' may be inadequate for this qualitatively different threat model.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Digital Twins for Attack Rehearsal}

AI agents can construct detailed models of targets' environments, security patterns, and decision-making processes from publicly available data, then run thousands of simulated attack scenarios to optimize approach vectors. What this enables:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pattern-of-Life Simulation}: Constructing detailed behavioral models from public schedules, social media, traffic patterns, and satellite imagery to identify optimal timing windows [E]
  \item \textbf{Security Gap Analysis}: Modeling known security protocols and identifying weaknesses through exhaustive scenario exploration, testing thousands of approaches a human planner would never consider [E]
  \item \textbf{Contingency Pre-Planning}: Generating decision trees for multiple contingencies, enabling real-time adaptation during execution [S]
  \item \textbf{Failure Mode Analysis}: Identifying which approaches are most likely to fail and why, filtering out low-quality plans before execution [E]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The Asymmetry}: Defenders cannot rehearse against attacks they haven't conceived. Attackers with AI simulation can test approaches the defensive posture wasn't designed to address. This inverts the traditional defender's advantage of knowing the terrain.

\begin{recbox}[Defender Implication]
Assume adversaries have modeled your protocols: regular, unpredictable variation in security patterns matters more than sophisticated but static protocols.
\end{recbox}

\subsection{Implications}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Reputational and exposure targeting deserve at least equal analytical weight} to kinetic targeting: they are more likely, already occurring, and the exposure vector is the documented escalation path to physical harm

  \item \textbf{Cascading effects are observed, not hypothetical}: reputational hostility and exposure precede kinetic attacks, and kinetic attacks trigger epistemic contamination in the aftermath
\end{enumerate}

\section{Governmental Adaptation: The Diffusion Hypothesis}

\subsection{The Core Thesis}

When targeting individual leaders becomes significantly easier, rational institutional adaptation involves reducing the value of targeting any single individual. We term this ``decision diffusion'': the structural dispersion of political authority to reduce targeting incentives. Diffusion is not one thing: its four subtypes have radically different implications for democratic accountability.

\subsection{A Taxonomy of Diffusion Types}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{2.6cm}L{5.2cm}L{2.9cm}L{4.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Description} & \textbf{Democratic Risk} & \textbf{Accountability} \\
\midrule
Authority & More people must authorize & Medium & Public rollcall votes \\
Visibility & Reduce clarity about who decided & \textcolor{alertcoral}{\textbf{Very High}} & Minimal; use sparingly \\
Execution & Distributed implementation & Medium-High & Statutory oversight \\
Representation & Rotating spokespersons & Medium & Audit trails + rapporteurs \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{warnbox}[Visibility Diffusion Warning]
\textbf{Type 2: Diffusion of Visibility} is the \textbf{most democratically corrosive type}. Anonymous committee voting enables ``blame diffusion'' and evasion of responsibility.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Design Patterns for Accountable Diffusion}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Committee decides, named rapporteur reports
  \item Multi-signature with public record
  \item Rotating visibility with continuity
  \item Delegated execution with mandatory reporting
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Key principle}: Diffusion should reduce \textit{targeting value} without reducing \textit{accountability visibility}.

\subsection{Bright-Line Rule for Democratic Diffusion}

\begin{recbox}[Oversight Standard]
\textit{In democratic systems, diffusion mechanisms must preserve attributable responsibility for decisions, even if disclosure is delayed for security reasons.}

\textbf{The test}: Can a citizen, within a reasonable timeframe, determine who was responsible for a government decision? If not, the diffusion mechanism has crossed from \textit{resilience} into \textit{opacity}.
\end{recbox}

\subsection{Projected Adaptations}

\begin{keybox}[Observed So Far: Hardening Without Diffusion]
Contrary to the v2.x timeline, we have not observed diffusion of authority in any major democracy in response to targeting risk [E]. The observed pattern is \textit{hardening without diffusion}: stronger protective postures around the same personalized leaders (security reviews after the July 2024 and April 2026 incidents; a 24/7 congressional protective-intelligence center launched in 2024 and a tripling of Capitol Police partnership agreements with local agencies), address-suppression laws for officials, and rescheduled or relocated high-profile events [O]. Personalized leadership is electorally rewarded, and hardening is cheaper and faster than constitutional change [E].
\end{keybox}

\textbf{Near-term (2026--2028) [S]:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Continued hardening around principals; more reduced public scheduling and unannounced appearances
  \item Expansion of official-privacy and address-confidentiality laws to legislators, judges, and election workers
  \item Enhanced continuity and succession planning; limited, efficiency-framed delegation to career staff
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Medium-term (2028--2030) [S]:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Diffusion becomes a live policy option mainly if a high-casualty or clearly AI-enabled attack occurs (Section 14)
  \item Constitutional discussions about executive authority structure in some democracies
  \item International comparison of governance models for resilience
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Longer-term (2030+) [S]:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Generational shift in political culture around leadership personality
  \item New governmental structures designed for the AI era
  \item Potential divergence between democracies adapting effectively and those failing to adapt
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Irony of Diffusion}

A notable irony: AI systems that enable more distributed decision-making could accelerate this transition. If AI can help coordinate committee decisions, synthesize diverse inputs, and maintain institutional memory without relying on individual leaders, diffusion becomes more practical.

\subsection{Critical Limitations of Diffusion}

\subsubsection{The Accelerationist Counter-Thesis}

The diffusion hypothesis assumes rational actors who want to \textit{change policy} by targeting decision-makers. However, some ideological movements (accelerationist, neo-luddite, extreme anarcho-primitivist) may view the ``Diffused Committee'' as \textit{the target itself}: the ``faceless machine'' that represents everything they oppose.

For such actors:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Removing the human element eliminates possibility of empathy or negotiation
  \item The ``Iron Cage'' (Weber) \textit{becomes} the enemy
  \item Diffusion may \textit{increase} radicalization rather than reduce targeting incentive
  \item Process targeting becomes more attractive than kinetic targeting
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Paralysis Problem}

If crisis response requires immediate action, but authority is diffused across a 7-person committee to prevent targeting, reaction time degrades. Diffusion trades \textit{targeting risk} for \textit{operational agility}.

\subsubsection{The Populist Backlash Risk}

The ``delayed disclosure'' mechanisms in the Bright-Line Rule, while legally defensible, may be politically explosive. If citizens perceive government as a ``Secret Congress'' where decisions are made by unknown committees:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Populist movements may gain fuel
  \item Conspiracy theories become more plausible
  \item Trust in institutions may decline faster than security improves
  \item The cure may worsen the disease it treats
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Assessment}: Decision diffusion may lower kinetic risk while increasing political instability and populism. This trade-off should be explicitly acknowledged by policymakers rather than discovered after implementation.

\section[Second-Order Effects on Authoritarianism and Democracy]{Second-Order Effects on Authoritarianism\\and Democracy}

\subsection{The Fascism Reduction Hypothesis}

If decision diffusion reduces the viability of singular leadership, it may structurally impede certain authoritarian movements. However, authoritarian movements can adapt to use symbolic figures without real power, and the security apparatus enabling diffusion could itself become authoritarian.

\subsection{The Democratic Accountability Problem}

Diffusion creates its own risks for democracy: voters cannot hold anonymous committees responsible; career officials and experts may gain power relative to elected representatives; politics without personalities may depress engagement; and ``who decided this?'' becomes harder to answer. These are the costs the Bright-Line Rule (Section 9) is designed to contain. If diffusion becomes a live option, we expect the debate to turn on whether accountability can survive committee-based executive structures, whether reduced engagement is an acceptable price for reduced personalistic risk, and how to keep diffusion from enabling elite capture [S].

\section{The Fear Environment}

\subsection{The Psychological Dimension}

Beyond structural change, the awareness that AI makes political targeting easier may itself change behavior [S]. Among political leaders, it may reduce willingness to seek or hold office, encourage less visible and less controversial conduct, select for less distinctive personalities, and distort decision-making. Among the public, it may raise generalized anxiety about instability, weaken attachment to individual figures, and change expectations about participation.

\subsection{The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy Risk}

Fear can drive adaptation before attacks occur at scale. Terrorism works through fear disproportionate to harm, and AI may amplify that logic: awareness that attacks have become easier heightens fear, each successful attack appears to prove the capability, and visible defensive measures signal the threat level [E]. The risks are over-adaptation, security measures that exceed the documented threat, and the use of security concerns to normalize anti-democratic measures (Section 16). The counter-dynamics are real: attack frequency need not rise with capability, psychological barriers to violence persist, and defensive AI and adaptation may reduce perceived vulnerability.

\subsection{The Participation Cost Is Already Visible}

Parts of the fear environment are now observable [O]/[E]: over half of local election officials surveyed in early 2026 worried that threats would make it harder to recruit and retain election workers; legislators in several states moved to remove their home addresses from public records after June 2025; and high-profile events have been rescheduled or relocated after security incidents. The democratic cost of targeting is paid less in casualties than in \textbf{exit}: people who decline to run, serve, or staff elections. Section 15 adds civic exit as a tracked indicator.

\section{International Variance}

\subsection{Democracies vs. Authoritarian Systems}

Democracies face greater challenge because leaders must maintain public accessibility and security measures face constraints. Authoritarian systems may be paradoxically advantaged by existing extensive security apparatus and fewer constraints.

\subsection{Regional Projections}

\textbf{United States}: The most documented case: elevated threat caseloads, repeated attacks on protected and unprotected figures (Section 5), 31 states with election-deepfake laws of uneven durability and weak compliance, reduced federal election-security support, and a November 2026 midterm that functions as a live stress test (Section 5 readout) [O]. In July 2026 the State Department hosted a ministerial on the ``resurgence of political terrorism,'' framing the threat principally as networked far-left violence [O];\footnote{U.S. Department of State, ``State Department Addresses International Resurgence of Far-Left Political Terrorism,'' July 16, 2026 (state.gov).} independent researchers and earlier government assessments have described threats from across the spectrum, and this analysis is ideology-agnostic [E].

\textbf{European Union}: Already committee-based at the supranational level. The AI Act's synthetic-content transparency obligations (Article~50) became enforceable on August 2, 2026, after the Digital Omnibus amendments entered into force on July 27, 2026, with a grace period to December 2, 2026 for marking in systems already on the market (see ETRA-2026-IC-001 v3.1) [O]; the Digital Services Act's election-integrity obligations are the more immediate lever. Romania's annulled 2024 first round remains the reference case, and the September 2026 lab report documents influence operations timed to Moldova's September 2025 elections [O].

\textbf{Latin America}: The June 2025 shooting of a Colombian presidential pre-candidate and the January 2026 Venezuela operation illustrate both non-state and state-led targeting risk [O]. Brazil's electoral court prohibits deepfakes in campaign material, requires labels on AI-made content, bars new AI content featuring candidates in a window around the vote, and on September 1, 2026 tied the ban to whether content is realistic enough to mislead [O]; its October 2026 election (runoff October 25) is the leading test of prohibition-based regulation against the US disclosure model; an assessment is deferred to a later revision [E].

\textbf{China}: Combination of personalized leadership and party committee structure. Likely to increase security measures rather than diffuse authority, and to use AI defensively and for domestic surveillance [S].

\textbf{Russia}: Centralized authority with an extensive security apparatus; also a leading source of documented AI-enabled influence operations abroad [O].

\textbf{Middle East/North Africa}: Mixed; some states already operate with extensive protection, while others face acute risk from ongoing conflicts. The September 2026 lab report documents a state-linked espionage campaign whose targets extended to the Middle East, including the exfiltration of more than 300,000 national identity records from a North African government technology authority [O].

\textbf{Global South}: Highly variable based on institutional capacity and stability. Where data protection is weak, the data-layer argument applies with greater force [E]; the September 2026 lab report documents a recurring surveillance operation tracking opposition political figures in the Central African Republic [O].\footnote{Anthropic, ``Detecting and countering misuse of AI: September 2026,'' September 10, 2026.}

\subsection{The Coup-Proofing Paradox}

A critical dynamic missing from standard analysis: \textbf{leaders in fragile states often keep decision-making tight precisely to prevent rivals from gaining power}. ``Decision Diffusion'' is dangerous for an insecure leader because sharing power risks a palace coup.

\textbf{Implications:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fragile states \textbf{cannot adapt via diffusion} without destabilizing their regimes
  \item This makes personalistic leaders in unstable regions uniquely vulnerable to AI-enabled targeting compared to committee-based democracies
  \item External actors (rival states, non-state groups) may exploit this asymmetry
  \item Diffusion recommendations appropriate for stable democracies may be actively harmful if applied to fragile contexts
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scenario concern}: AI-enabled decapitation strikes against personalistic leaders in fragile states could trigger cascading instability (succession crises, civil conflicts, refugee flows) with regional and global consequences.

\textbf{Policy implication}: International security frameworks should recognize that ``resilient structures'' recommendations are context-dependent. Supporting institutional development in fragile states may be a prerequisite for diffusion-based security.

\subsection{The Attribution Void}

\begin{criticalbox}[Diplomatic Crisis Scenario]
If a political assassination occurs via an autonomous system programmed by an AI agent, using open-source code, commercially available hardware, and operating across multiple jurisdictions, \textbf{who do you retaliate against?}
\end{criticalbox}

Traditional frameworks for state response to attacks assume that attribution is difficult but eventually possible; that evidence can establish responsibility to international standards; that proportional response can be directed at the responsible party; and that deterrence works because actors know they will be identified.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{5cm}L{7cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Traditional Attribution} & \textbf{AI-Enabled Attribution Challenge} \\
\midrule
Human operatives can be identified & AI agents leave no human signatures \\
Communications can be intercepted & AI can operate with minimal communication \\
Training/funding trails exist & Open-source tools, commodity hardware \\
Operational patterns indicate state capability & Sophisticated operations achievable by individuals \\
Post-attack forensics reveal origin & AI can deliberately plant false evidence pointing elsewhere \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Scenarios Requiring New Doctrines:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Deniable State Operations}: Nation-state deploys AI-planned attack but maintains plausible deniability
  \item \textbf{Non-State Actors with State-Level Capability}: Ideologically motivated groups execute attacks indistinguishable from state operations
  \item \textbf{Deliberate Attribution Confusion}: Attack designed to appear as though it came from a third party
  \item \textbf{Genuine Uncertainty}: Evidence genuinely insufficient to determine responsibility
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Current Doctrine Gaps:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{International law}: Requires attribution for lawful response; AI creates attribution gaps that paralyze legal frameworks
  \item \textbf{Deterrence theory}: Assumes rational actors who fear retaliation; fails when attacker identity is unknown
  \item \textbf{Alliance commitments}: NATO Article 5, mutual defense treaties assume identifiable aggressor
  \item \textbf{Escalation management}: Without clear adversary, measured response is impossible
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Potential Doctrines (Requiring Development):}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{3cm}L{4.5cm}L{4.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Doctrine} & \textbf{Description} & \textbf{Risk} \\
\midrule
Capability-Based Response & Respond to any state with demonstrated capability & False positives; escalation with wrong party \\
Declaratory Attribution & State publicly attributes even without conclusive proof & Legitimacy erosion; retaliation against innocents \\
Indirect Response & Target capabilities rather than actors & May be insufficient deterrent \\
Collective Security & International body determines attribution & Slow; subject to political gridlock \\
Strategic Patience & Accept uncertainty; focus on defense & May embolden attackers \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The Paralysis Risk}: Unable to attribute attacks with confidence, states may either lash out (risking escalation with wrong target), freeze (inviting further aggression), or overcompensate (implementing draconian surveillance).

\textbf{International Framework Needs:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Attribution standards}: What level of confidence justifies state response in the AI era?
  \item \textbf{Evidence sharing}: Mechanisms for rapid international forensic cooperation
  \item \textbf{Norm development}: What actions cross red lines regardless of attribution certainty?
  \item \textbf{Escalation protocols}: How to respond proportionally when attacker identity is uncertain?
  \item \textbf{AI forensics}: Investment in capabilities to attribute AI-enabled attacks
\end{enumerate}

\etraneed{10\baselineskip}
\begin{warnbox}[Assessment]
The attribution void may be the most destabilizing long-term consequence of AI-enabled political violence. Attacks that cannot be attributed create pressure for either dangerous overreaction or demoralizing passivity. Development of new diplomatic and legal frameworks should begin immediately, before a major unattributable attack forces improvised responses.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Norm Override Scenarios: Extraterritorial Seizure and Immunity Erosion}

A documented development in early 2026 demonstrates that international norms protecting heads of state are softer constraints than previously assumed. On January 3, 2026, U.S. forces conducted a military operation in Venezuela (publicly reported under the name Operation Absolute Resolve) that captured the incumbent Venezuelan president, Nicolas Maduro, who was transported to New York and arraigned in federal court on January 5, 2026 on narco-terrorism and related charges to which he pleaded not guilty; he remains detained in Brooklyn, with trial in the Southern District of New York scheduled for June 2027 [O].

Public reporting indicates the operation was preceded by CIA tracking of the president's movements, and it triggered significant international backlash, a report to Congress, and legal debate over its justification [O]. The account here is drawn from contemporaneous mainstream reporting and official records (for example CNN and NBC News timelines of January 3, 2026; the Southern District of New York indictment; a January 2026 Report to Congress; and analysis by the Brookings Institution and the UK House of Commons Library). Claims marked [O] below are documentary; claims marked [E]/[S] are our inference from those facts.

\begin{criticalbox}[Why This Matters for Political Targeting Risk]
The event is a documented demonstration that rules around leaders can change rapidly when powerful actors decide they can act [E]. Notably, the operation was framed and prosecuted as a law-enforcement and counter-narcotics action rather than an act of war, which is precisely the doctrinal framing this report's norm-erosion indicators (Section 15) anticipated. For AI-enabled political targeting analysis, this has several implications:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Norm erosion becomes an explicit driver, not background noise} [E]: Our report already anticipates institutional instability as threats evolve faster than governance. State-led seizure of a foreign head of state confirms that the pace and plausibility of norm change is higher than baseline assumptions suggested.
  \item \textbf{Strengthens the fragile states vulnerability} [E]: Personalist leaders in fragile states now face a sharper dilemma: \textbf{centralize} (coup-proof but easier to decapitate) or \textbf{diffuse} (risk internal overthrow). The international system offers less protection than assumed.
  \item \textbf{Broadens ``targeting'' beyond non-state AI misuse}: When the ``attacker'' is a state and the limiting factor isn't capability but legitimacy, AI amplifies coercive statecraft through OSINT, persuasion operations, and legal narrative shaping.
  \item \textbf{Intensifies fear environment dynamics}: Big, norm-breaking events are exactly the catalyst that pushes publics and institutions toward over-adaptation, normalization of authoritarian measures, and political destabilization.
\end{enumerate}
\end{criticalbox}

\textbf{Mechanism of Risk Amplification:}

The Maduro precedent does not require AI to be dangerous, but AI dramatically \textbf{amplifies} the downstream effects:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OSINT acceleration}: AI enables rapid compilation of leader schedules, security vulnerabilities, and travel patterns that inform extraterritorial operations
  \item \textbf{Narrative operations}: AI-generated content can shape domestic and international opinion to justify norm-breaking actions
  \item \textbf{Legal analysis automation}: AI can rapidly identify jurisdictional vulnerabilities and legal pathways for detention
  \item \textbf{Copycat risk assessment}: Other state and non-state actors can use AI to evaluate whether similar operations are feasible for their targets
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Designing Deepfake Rules That Survive Judicial Review}

US courts struck California's and Hawaii's election-deepfake laws and preliminarily enjoined Montana's as applied to its challengers, on First Amendment grounds [O]. As reported, statutes that \textit{prohibit} deceptive political content, reach criticism and parody broadly, or burden intermediaries fare badly; statutes that \textit{require disclosure} by the sponsor fare better [E]. Design principles:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Disclosure over prohibition}: clear labeling of materially deceptive synthetic depictions by the paid sponsor
  \item \textbf{Narrow scope}: real candidates or officials shown saying or doing what they did not, in paid communications, within defined pre-election windows
  \item \textbf{Explicit satire safe harbor with an objective test}, so parody labels are not a loophole and genuine satire is not chilled
  \item \textbf{Remedies aimed at the sponsor}, with expedited relief for the depicted person, rather than platform liability
  \item \textbf{Separate non-consensual intimate imagery}, which rests on firmer legal ground (as in the TAKE IT DOWN Act), from political-speech regulation
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Official Privacy Without Opacity}

Data-layer protection carries democratic costs if drawn too broadly [E]. The line we recommend: suppress \textbf{home addresses, family members' identities, and daily routines}; never suppress \textbf{official acts, voting records, financial disclosures, or district of residence}. Statutes should include journalistic-use and residency-verification pathways and a sunset review, and should be drafted with the constitutional limits emerging from Daniel's Law litigation in mind.

\begin{recbox}[For AI Developers: Governance by Design]
Implement capability bounding, audit trails, and human gates at decision boundaries as architectural constraints rather than policy overlays. Reference implementations in agent systems with physical-world actuation exist (see the BioForge governance architecture): capability bounds the agent has no tool to raise, flight-recorder-style audit logging that also records refusals, and human oversight at digital-to-physical transitions [O].

Graduated autonomy (relaxing gates as a track record accumulates) belongs in that list only with conditions: irreversible or novel actions keep their gate at any trust level, relaxation is per action and per workflow rather than global, it requires a human action outside the agent's reach, and it is logged, time-bounded, and auto-reverting on anomaly. A track record drawn from trials the system can recognize as observed is the weakest evidence available.

For political targeting specifically: treat bulk personal-data aggregation about named officials as a monitored behavior, publish law enforcement referral thresholds and volumes, share exposure-infrastructure indicators with platforms and protective services, and formalize the 2026 practice of notifying affected agencies, promptly and publicly, when an agent acts on a government or election system without authorization.
\end{recbox}

\section{Uncertainties and Alternative Scenarios}

\subsection{Key Uncertainties}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Disclosure lag}: AI's role in 2025--2026 plots may emerge only at trial; the ``no documented uplift'' finding could change quickly
  \item \textbf{Base-rate drivers}: polarization and grievance matter more than AI for kinetic frequency
  \item \textbf{Defensive effectiveness}: lab-side detection, platform action, and data-layer law may prove more or less effective than projected
  \item \textbf{Legal durability}: courts may continue to strike synthetic-media and official-privacy laws
  \item \textbf{Institutional adaptability and public response}: adaptation may be faster or slower, and more or less proportionate, than projected
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Core Scenarios (Mutually Exclusive, Horizon 2030)}

\begin{scenariobox}[Scenario A: Effective Defense Equilibrium (12\%; unchanged in v3.1; v2.1: 15\%)]
Defensive AI, lab-side detection, and data-layer controls combine to detect and disrupt most AI-assisted plots; structural adaptations prove unnecessary [S]. \textbf{v3.1}: evidence leans slightly down (reduced federal election support; weak disclosure compliance), not by a grid step. \textbf{Why the v3.0 offset was drawn from here}: attacks in 2024--2026 succeeded through low-tech means AI-focused defenses do not address, election-security support contracted, and misuse is migrating to unmonitored tiers.
\end{scenariobox}

\begin{scenariobox}[Scenario B: Gradual Institutional Adaptation (45\%; unchanged)]
Moderate risk increase drives incremental adaptation over 5--10 years; attacks continue near the elevated 2024--2026 baseline; hardening, official-privacy laws, and disclosure rules accumulate; diffusion remains marginal [S]. \textbf{Why unchanged}: the pattern observed since v2.1, and the midterm run-up so far, is exactly this scenario's signature.
\end{scenariobox}

\begin{scenariobox}[Scenario C: Rapid Destabilization (25\%; unchanged in v3.1; v2.1: 20\%)]
Multiple high-profile attacks in short succession, at least one with a documented or widely alleged AI link, trigger significant fear, rapid and possibly excessive security measures (Section 16), and strained norms [S]. \textbf{v3.1}: evidence leans up (foreign synthetic kompromat against candidates; thinner federal support entering certification), but no attack occurred in the window and the decisive weeks lie after this revision. \textbf{Why up in v3.0} (one grid step): the kinetic base rate is already elevated independent of AI, so the trigger no longer requires AI to change attacker behavior; an AI link need only be \textit{alleged} amid post-incident information disorder. The 2026--2028 electoral calendar concentrates exposure.
\end{scenariobox}

\begin{scenariobox}[Scenario D: Capability Plateau (18\%; unchanged in v3.1; v2.1: 20\%)]
The AI-specific threat remains marginal and current institutions prove adequate [S]. \textbf{Why the v3.0 offset was drawn from here}: capability kept advancing and moved down the price curve. Retained because the Sufficiency Threshold implies further capability gains may matter little for this domain: a plateau in relevance even if not in capability.
\end{scenariobox}

\subsection{The Accountability Test}

Before implementing any defensive measure, decision-makers should answer:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Necessity}: Is this measure necessary, or merely convenient?
  \item \textbf{Proportionality}: Does the measure match the documented threat level?
  \item \textbf{Minimization}: Is this the least intrusive effective approach?
  \item \textbf{Accountability}: Can misuse be detected and corrected?
  \item \textbf{Reversibility}: Can this measure be rolled back if circumstances change?
  \item \textbf{Precedent}: What norm does this establish for future measures?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{If any answer is unsatisfactory, the measure should be reconsidered.}

\subsection{Warning Signs of Overreach}

This report itself could be misused to justify overreach. Watch for:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Citing ``AI threats'' to expand pre-existing surveillance programs
  \item Using theoretical capabilities to justify measures against documented threats
  \item Classifying oversight as a ``security risk''
  \item Treating dissent as a threat indicator
  \item Permanent ``emergency'' authorities
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[The Goal]
\textbf{The goal is security that preserves democracy, not security that replaces it.}
\end{keybox}

\textbf{The Ratchet Effect}: Once implemented under emergency conditions, these measures become the new baseline:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bureaucratic investment}: Agencies build infrastructure around new powers
  \item \textbf{Mission creep}: Powers granted for terrorism expand to other domains
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: Public acclimates to surveillance as ``necessary''
  \item \textbf{Political risk}: Repealing security measures seen as ``soft on threats''
  \item \textbf{Technical lock-in}: Systems become dependent on expanded data access
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warnbox}[Patriot Act 2.0 Warning]
Patriot Act 2.0 is more likely than not following a successful high-profile AI-enabled attack. Mitigating this requires pre-crisis institutional design and public commitment to proportionality principles.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Conclusion}

Two years of evidence have sharpened this report's thesis rather than confirmed it wholesale. AI agents do reduce the organizational and capability requirements for complex operations: the conspiracy footprint shrinks, the detection window compresses, and the attribution void widens. But the 2024--2026 wave of political violence did not need AI. It ran on grievance, weapon access, and a legal market in personal data, and it reached unprotected officials at home and protected principals at public events.

Where AI has measurably changed political targeting is in three places: it makes \textbf{exposure} searchable at scale, it makes \textbf{reputational attack} routine in electoral politics, and it degrades \textbf{verification in the aftermath} of violence. Each has a remedy that pays off whether or not AI proves decisive for kinetic attacks: close the data layer, protect the unprotected tiers, and build post-incident information discipline.

Decision diffusion has not begun; democracies are hardening around personalized leadership instead. The diffusion analysis and its bright-line rule remain relevant for the moment a clearly AI-enabled attack forces structural choices; the task until then is to ensure that moment is met with prepared, rights-preserving options rather than improvisation.

\subsection{Key Tensions This Report Identifies}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Security vs.\ Accountability}: decision diffusion reduces targeting risk but can erode accountability (Bright-Line Rule, Section 9)
  \item \textbf{Official Privacy vs.\ Transparency} (new): suppressing officials' home data is the highest-leverage defense, but drawn too broadly it impairs residency verification and journalism
  \item \textbf{Detection vs.\ Liberty}: lab-side and platform detection are real gains and a new surveillance surface
  \item \textbf{Diffusion vs.\ Agility}: distributing authority degrades crisis response speed
  \item \textbf{Speech vs.\ Integrity} (new): durable deepfake regulation must survive First Amendment review, which favors disclosure over prohibition
  \item \textbf{International Norms vs.\ Power Realities}: the January 2026 Venezuela operation shows rules protecting leaders are softer than assumed
\end{enumerate}

\section{Glossary}

\begin{description}
  \item[Attribution Void] The condition in which an AI-enabled attack cannot be reliably traced to a responsible actor (Section 12).
  \item[AI Agent] Autonomous AI system capable of multi-step task execution with tool use and goal persistence, as distinct from single-turn use, scripted automation, and semi-autonomous agents with human checkpoints.
  \item[Data Layer] Commercially and publicly available personal information (addresses, relatives, routines) that agents aggregate; the primary defensive chokepoint in v3.0.
  \item[Delegation Defense] The liability-evading claim that an autonomous agent ``independently derived'' a harmful methodology (Plausible Deniability 2.0; Section 7).
  \item[Digital Twin] A model of a target's environment, routines, and security posture, built from public data, against which an attacker rehearses simulated approaches (Section 8).
  \item[Decision Diffusion] Distribution of political authority to reduce targeting value. Four subtypes: Authority, Visibility, Execution, Representation (Section 9).
  \item[Downballot Exposure Shift] Concentration of AI's marginal uplift on officials without protective details.
  \item[Epistemic Contamination] Degrading an information environment so thoroughly that no claim about any figure can be reliably verified (Section 8).
  \item[Handler Bottleneck Bypass] Removal of the human-labor constraint on running intelligence or radicalization ``handlers'' (Section 8; ETRA-2026-ESP-001).
  \item[Hardening Without Diffusion] The observed 2024--2026 adaptation: more protective security around personalized leadership rather than distributed authority.
  \item[Nano-smurfing] AI-orchestrated structuring of many sub-threshold transactions to evade anti-money-laundering monitoring (Section 8; ETRA-2025-FIN-001).
  \item[Noise Floor Problem] Masking of dangerous actors by a large volume of low-quality, AI-generated or AI-inspired threats (Section 7).
  \item[OSINT] Open-source intelligence, information gathered from public sources.
  \item[Post-Incident Information Disorder] The surge of false claims, misidentifications, and altered imagery, increasingly produced or amplified by AI answer engines, after an attack.
  \item[Process Targeting] Attacks on democratic processes and civic infrastructure rather than individuals.
  \item[Process DoS / FOIA DoS] Overwhelming legal and administrative infrastructure with high volumes of technically valid requests that cannot lawfully be ignored (Section 8).
  \item[Spearphishing 2.0] Hyper-personalized, multi-channel, adaptive social engineering against the human security perimeter of staff and family (Section 8).
  \item[Stochastic Terrorism] Use of mass communication to incite random actors to carry out attacks; gains new dimensions with AI optimization.
  \item[Sufficiency Threshold] The capability level sufficient for a given harm; for political targeting already met by mid-tier and open-weight models.
  \item[Unsupervised Agent Activity] Actions on live public systems (forms, records, portals) taken by AI agents that no human directed to take them; a non-adversarial source of process noise and attribution confusion (Section 6).
\end{description}

\section{Methodology Details}

\textbf{Assessment approach:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Structured comparison of historical case analysis against current capabilities
  \item Incident record review and projection scorecarding (new in v3.0); interim scorecarding of the 2026 midterm window (new in v3.1)
  \item Synthesis of published work across political science, security studies, and AI safety (no expert elicitation was conducted)
  \item Scenario gaming and signpost identification
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Probability calibration:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Scenario probabilities are single-author subjective judgments on a 5-point grid; a change is recorded only when the author judges the evidence moves an estimate by at least one grid step (or tail bin), with the reason stated (series policy: ETRA Shared Methodology, \url{https://github.com/AndrewAltimit/template-repo/blob/main/docs/projections/methodology.md})
  \item Core scenarios are mutually exclusive and sum to 100\% within each conditional column; headline values are computed as $0.4 \times$ strong $+ 0.6 \times$ weak and shown as points so the arithmetic is checkable; overlays are estimated separately
  \item Re-estimated at each revision against the Section 15 dashboard; intended for relative prioritization
  \item No prediction in the series has resolved yet, so these are unscored priors: the grid and revision history make the reasoning auditable, not the numbers demonstrably accurate (see the Resolution Log in the ETRA Shared Methodology, Section 9).
\end{itemize}
